Introduction

Probabilities, confidence intervals, and margins of error are mathematical concepts that help make better predictions and comparisons. These valuable quantitative tools are part of the Quantitative Reasoning Process.

Probability

To make an accurate prediction or comparison, you must understand the likelihood that a certain outcome will occur.

The probability of an event is a measurement between 0 and 1 of how likely an event is to happen. An event with a probability of 0 will never happen. An event with a probability of 1 is certain to happen.

Although some events have a probability of 0 (such as rolling a 7 on a standard six-sided die) or a probability of 1 (like the sun rising in the morning), most events have a probability somewhere between 0 and 1.

Probabilities are always shown as a number between 0 and 1 and are written as percentages. For example, 0.4 is the same as 40%. In other words, if the probability of something happening is 0.4, there is a 40% chance it could happen.

You have likely encountered probabilities often enough to have an intuitive sense of what they mean. If there is a 90% chance it will rain today, it will probably rain. If there is only a 10% chance of rain, it will likely not rain.

There are several equivalent ways to think about or explain probabilities: If the probability of getting a cold is 0.3, you could say any of these:

  • There is a 30% chance you will get a cold.

  • Out of every 10 people, three will get a cold.

  • Out of a group of 100 people, around 30 of them will get a cold.

Informal Use of Probabilities

Probabilities are used in everyday life without us even realizing it. Imagine you go out to take a walk and notice that it is a dark, cloudy day with the possibility of rain. Without thinking too much, you know that if you go out without an umbrella, the chance of getting caught in a storm and getting yourself drenched is higher than if you account for the possibility of rain by carrying an umbrella. You quickly go back inside to retrieve your umbrella, just in case. You made this decision by comparing the probabilities and risks to yourself, even if you didn't consciously know it.

These informal probabilities are like assumptions we make when we use quantitative reasoning (which means using numbers and data to make decisions). When we assume something, there's a risk that it might not be true. The likelihood of our assumption being false affects the choices we make.

For example, think about deciding whether to take out a business loan or not. You might assume that the new business will make you rich. But deep down, you know that this assumption is not likely to be true. While some graduates in high-demand fields might get such high salaries, most graduates earn much less. In 2016, the average starting salary for new bachelor's degree holders in the United States was around $50,556. If you base your decision on the unlikely assumption of a $90,000 salary, you're taking a big risk, and it could lead to a bad choice.

When you create an assumption, it's crucial to think about how likely it is to be true. In the example above, a specific probability was not calculated; however, you could still tell that the likelihood of a $90,000 salary was low by comparing it to national salary data. This kind of informal assessment can be done by looking at historical information, studies, surveys, and other data that help you estimate how likely your assumption is to be true.

Remember, the decisions you make using quantitative reasoning are only as good as your assumptions. If you base them on unlikely assumptions, then the chance of making a good decision becomes low too. It's essential to be thoughtful and consider the probabilities behind your assumptions to make better choices.

Empirical and Theoretical Probabilities

There are two types of probabilities: empirical and theoretical .

Empirical probability is based on what has happened in the past or when you try an event many times to see how often the desired outcome occurs.

Theoretical probability, on the other hand, uses your knowledge of the situation and math formulas to predict how often the desired outcome will happen in the future.

In Skylar's case, he used empirical probabilities because he calculated based on what customers had bought before.

A common example of theoretical probability is a coin toss. Since there are two sides (heads and tails) and the coin is fair, there's a 50% chance of getting either heads or tails. This is theoretical because you don’t actually toss the coin multiple times to find out. You know it based on the structure of the coin.

When calculating empirical probabilities, it's essential to have a large number of trials. For example, if you toss a coin 10 times and record the results, you can get a better idea of the probabilities.

The result is 7 tails and 3 heads. This is not unusual, even for an equally weighted, fair coin. However, if this outcome is used to compute an empirical probability there is a 70% chance of getting tails and a 30% chance of getting heads. This isn’t even close to the theoretical probability you would expect.

For an empirical probability to be accurate, it must be based on a large number of trials.

Simple, classic examples, like coins and dice, can help you understand probability.

Estimating with Confidence

Here is an example based on voting and elections: A news report states that 51% of the voters are going to vote for Andrews. But this percentage is based on a sample. The news agency has not contacted every single registered voter and asked them how they plan to vote. They only contacted some of the voters. The news agency knows the answer based on their sample could be different from the true percentage for all the registered voters, the entire population. So in addition to giving the percentage of voters planning to vote for Andrews, the news report also gives a margin of error.

Statisticians distinguish between two types of numbers: parameters and statistics.

A parameter is a number based on a survey of the entire population.
A statistic is a number based on a survey of just a sample of the population.

When you see a percentage like "51% of voters" in the news, it's a statistic. It is based on a sample of the population, not the entire group. Statistics are used because it's challenging and expensive to ask every person in a big population. So, researchers contact only a smaller group (sample) and make an estimate from that.

Since it is only a sample, the statistic might not be exactly the same as the true percentage of the whole population (parameter). That's where the margin of error comes in.

Statistics help estimate the true percentage (parameter) for the entire population. The margin of error tells how much the statistic could differ from the true percentage.

For example, assume the margin of error for the voter study is 3%. By subtracting and adding the margin of error from the sample percentage (51%), you get a range (interval) of numbers. You can be quite confident that the true percentage of voters falls within this interval. This range of numbers is called a confidence interval.

95% Confidence Intervals

News organizations and polling companies often report a statistic along with the margin of error. You can use this information to find the confidence interval.

$$ \text{confidence interval}=\text{the statistic ± the margin of error} $$

Example 4: Grocery Bags

Grocery Bags

The Gallup organization took a survey in March 2017, in which they asked 1,018 adults in the US (aged 18 and older): "Which of these is less harmful to the environment: using paper shopping bags or using plastic shopping bags?"

They found that 73% of the surveyed adults believed that paper bags are better for the environment than plastic bags. This 73% is a statistic because it's based on the opinions of a group of adults, not all adults in the US (which would be the parameter).

The margin of sampling error is 4 percentage points at a 95% confidence level. This margin of error helps calculate a confidence interval, which is a range of percentages that can confidently state the true percentage of all US adults who believe paper bags are better for the environment than plastic bags.

$$ \text{confidence interval=the statistic}\pm\text{the margin of error} $$
$$ =73\% \pm 4\% $$
$$ =(73\%-4\%,73\%+4\%) $$
$$ =(69\%, 77\%) $$

This shows our 95% confidence interval is (69%,77%). This means we are 95% certain that the true percentage of adults in the United States who think using paper grocery bags is better for the environment than using plastic grocery bags is between 69% and 77%.

Computing 95% Confidence Intervals

In the previous examples, you were given the margin of error, which helped find the confidence interval. But what if you don't have the margin of error?

You can use a simplified formula to estimate the percentage of a population with a certain characteristic based on a sample. The formula is not as complex as the one used by statisticians, but it still gives a good approximation and helps us understand confidence intervals and margin of error better.

To use the formula, you need two variables:

x represents the number of people in the sample with the characteristic you are interested in.

n represents the total number of people in the sample.

$$ \text{Confidence Interval}=\frac{x}{n}\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt n} $$
$$ \text{Where Margin of Error} = \frac{1}{\sqrt n} $$

The following examples show how to use this formula to compute a confidence interval.

Example 7: Election Results

Election Results

A school district is having a special election to pass a school bond. The purpose of the bond is to get money for building a new elementary school. To pay for the bond, they will collect extra property taxes over the next 10 years.

To see if people support the bond, the local newspaper asked 200 registered voters how they will vote on it. Out of those 200, 72 said they will vote in favor of the bond.

Solution

Find a 95% confidence interval for this situation using a formula.

$$ \text{Confidence Interval}=\frac{x}{n}\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt n} $$
$$ =\frac{72}{200}\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{200}} $$
$$ =0.36\pm0.07 $$
$$ =(0.36-0.07, 0.36+0.07) $$
$$ =(0.29, 0.43) $$

This confidence interval gives a 95% certainty that the true percentage of voters who will vote in favor of the bond is between 29% and 43%. From this sample, it appears that the bond will fail to be approved.

Among graduates in the survey with no student loan debt, 40% were thriving financially. Because we know the margin of error was 1.4%, you can find the confidence interval:

$$ \text{Confidence Interval}=40\%\pm1.4\% $$
$$ =(40-1.4, 40+1.4) $$
$$ =(38.6,41.4) $$

You are 95% confident that the true percentage of graduates with not student loan debt who are thriving financially is between 38.6% and 41.4%.

Over $50,000 in Student Loan Debt

Among graduates in the survey with over $50,000 in student loan debt, 25% were thriving financially. Because the margin of error was 1.4%, you can find the confidence interval:

$$ \text{Confidence Interval}=25\%\pm1.4\% $$
$$ =(25-1.4, 25+1.4) $$
$$ =(23.6,26.4) $$

You are 95% confident that the true percentage of graduates with over $50,000 in student loan debt who are thriving financially is between 23.6% and 26.4%.

Among graduates in the survey with no student loan debt, 47% were thriving socially. Because the margin of error was 1.4%, we can find the confidence interval:

Be careful when comparing statistics obtained from a sample. It is easy to mistakenly conclude that because one statistic is larger than another statistic, the corresponding parameter is also larger than the other parameter. If the confidence intervals overlap, you do not have enough statistical evidence to say the parameters are different from one another.
