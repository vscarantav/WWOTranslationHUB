Chapter 3 Draft: Professional Email

Overview

  • Task: You will analyze a real communication situation, write a professional request email, and explain the rhetorical choices you made using Week 3 concepts.
  • Purpose: To practice writing clear, professional emails and reflect on how audience, purpose, and context shape your communication choices.
  • Length : 425–650 words total across all three parts

What This Assignment is Asking You to Do

This assignment has three parts. You will write a short analysis of your communication situation, then write the actual email, then look back and explain the choices you made. Each part builds on the one before it.

The situation you write about should be real. Think of a moment when you need to notify a supervisor, manager, team lead, or professional contact in advance about a conflict. If nothing comes to mind, consider these possibilities: notifying a supervisor that you will need to miss an upcoming work meeting, informing a manager about a scheduling conflict before it becomes a problem, or letting a professional contact know in advance that you will be unavailable for a planned commitment.

Read the worked example before you begin. It shows what all three parts look like when they are done well.

Professional Standard: What Workplace Email Expects

Before you write, it helps to understand what professional email communication requires in most workplace contexts worldwide. These standards vary somewhat by country and industry, but the following expectations are widely shared.

A professional email arrives early. When you know you will miss a meeting or be unable to fulfill a commitment, the expectation in most workplaces is that you communicate as soon as you know, not the morning of the meeting. Early notice gives your supervisor time to adjust the agenda, reassign a task, or find coverage. Last-minute notice, even when honest, creates the same disruption as no notice at all.

A professional email is specific about the conflict. Your supervisor does not need a detailed account of your personal situation, but they do need enough information to understand the scope of the problem. Telling a manager you will be “unavailable” on Thursday is less useful than telling them you have a medical appointment from 9:00 to 11:00 and will be reachable by phone after noon.

A professional email offers a path forward. Notifying someone of a conflict and stopping there puts the problem entirely on them to solve. A stronger message identifies what you will do to minimize the impact: reviewing meeting notes afterward, sending your contributions in advance, or asking a colleague to represent your update.

These standards apply whether you are writing in English or in your first language, and whether your workplace is in Lagos, Manila, São Paulo, or Salt Lake City. The specific tone and level of formality may shift by culture and context. Your rhetorical analysis in Part 3 is where you will reflect on those adjustments.

Example

The following example was written for this assignment. It is here to show you the format, the level of detail, and the kind of thinking each part requires. Your situation will be different, and your analysis should reflect your own choices.

Part 1: Situation Analysis (this student wrote 189 words)

My recipient is Ms. Priya Nair, the team lead for my department at the logistics company where I work part time. Our relationship is professional and respectful, but not close. She manages six people and runs our Monday morning coordination meetings. She communicates directly and values reliability. Based on how she has responded to team members in the past, I believe she will respond better to a message that is brief, specific, and solution-focused rather than one that offers a long personal explanation.

The situation is that I have a required medical appointment on Monday morning that overlaps with the team meeting. I have known about the appointment for one week, and I am writing on Friday afternoon so Ms. Nair has the weekend to adjust the agenda if needed.

Email is the right channel because Ms. Nair works across two locations and is rarely available for an immediate conversation. A written message also gives her a record she can refer to when planning the meeting. Timing matters because sending this on Friday gives her two days to prepare, which is meaningfully more respectful than sending it Monday morning.

Part 2: The Email (this student wrote 122 words):

Subject: Advance Notice: Unable to Attend Monday Morning Meeting

Dear Ms. Nair,

My name is Kofi Asante, and I am on your logistics coordination team. I am writing to let you know in advance that I will be unable to attend this Monday’s 9:00 a.m. team meeting. I have a required medical appointment that morning that I cannot reschedule.

I wanted to notify you as early as possible so you have time to adjust the agenda if needed. I will review the meeting notes as soon as they are available and follow up with any team member whose updates affect my current tasks. If there is anything you need from me before Monday, I am available by email through Sunday evening.

Thank you for your understanding.

Respectfully, Kofi Asante Logistics Coordination

Part 3: Rhetorical Analysis (this student wrote 276 words)

The appeal I emphasized most was ethos. My goal was to demonstrate professionalism before the conflict happened rather than after it, so almost every sentence is designed to show that I take my responsibilities seriously. Sending the notice on Friday rather than Monday morning is itself a rhetorical choice. It signals that I understand how my absence affects the team and that I planned ahead to reduce that impact. Saying “I will review the meeting notes as soon as they are available” reinforces the same message without claiming more than I can deliver.

I used logos in the middle section by giving Ms. Nair specific and actionable information: the time of the meeting I will miss, the reason in one phrase, and two concrete steps I will take afterward. My audience analysis told me she manages six people and does not have time to ask follow-up questions, so the message needed to answer them before she had to ask.

I kept pathos minimal. Mentioning a medical appointment is enough context. Explaining it further would have shifted the tone from professional notification to personal appeal, which is not appropriate for this relationship.

Kairos shaped the timing of the entire message. Sending advance notice on Friday, two full days before the meeting, is more respectful than sending it Monday morning. That timing is part of the message itself.

If I were writing to a close colleague rather than a team lead, I would use a less formal tone and skip the employee ID. In a workplace culture where seniority and hierarchy are emphasized more explicitly, I would open with a stronger expression of respect before stating my situation.

Step 1: Situation Analysis (150–200 words)

Describe your communication situation before you write the email. Answer these questions in paragraph form:

  • Who is your recipient? Describe their role and your relationship with them.
  • What do you need to communicate, and what outcome do you want from this email?
  • Why is email the right channel? Consider whether a phone call, an in-person conversation, or a message through another platform would be better, and explain why you chose email.
  • Is timing a factor? If so, explain how your timing choice reflects professional awareness.

Step 2: The Email (75–150 words)

Write your professional email using this format:

  • Subject line:
    • Use the pattern [Action or Topic] + [Specific Detail].
    • For example: “Advance Notice: Unable to Attend Monday Morning Meeting” or “Schedule Conflict: Thursday Client Call.”
  • Greeting: Address your recipient by name and title. “Dear Ms. [Last Name]” or “Dear [Full Name]” are both appropriate depending on your relationship and workplace culture.
  • Body, part 1: Identify yourself and give the context your reader needs. State what you are notifying them about and when.
  • Body, part 2: Explain the situation briefly and specifically. Give enough detail that your supervisor understands the scope of the conflict without requiring a follow-up message.
  • Body, part 3: Offer a path forward. State what you will do to minimize the impact of your absence. Close with a statement that confirms your continued reliability.
  • Closing: Use a professional sign-off (Respectfully, Sincerely, Best regards) followed by your full name and any identifying information your recipient will need, such as your job title, or team name.

Step 3: Rhetorical Analysis (200–250 words)

After you finish the email, look back at the choices you made and explain them.

  • Use Week 3 vocabulary. Cover these four areas:
    • Rhetorical appeals: Which appeal (ethos, pathos, logos, or kairos) did you emphasize most, and why was that the right choice for this recipient? Identify a specific sentence or phrase in your email where that appeal appears.
    • Audience influence: How did what you know about your recipient shape your word choice or tone? Give one specific example of a decision you made because of your audience analysis.
    • Effectiveness: What do you think is the strongest part of your email? Is there anything you are uncertain about?
    • Adaptation: How would you change this email if you were sending it to someone with a different role, a different relationship to you, or a different cultural or professional context?

Step 4: Requirements Checklist

Before you submit, confirm you have included:

  • Part 1: Situation analysis, 150–200 words, written in paragraph form
  • Part 2: A complete professional email with subject line, greeting, three-part body, and professional closing
  • Part 3: Rhetorical analysis, 200–250 words, using course vocabulary (ethos, pathos, logos, kairos, audience, purpose, channel)
  • A real situation from your own professional or workplace experience, not a hypothetical or the example above

English Writing: Mastering the Basics

Commas and Coordinating Adjectives

You can use commas to list both adjectives and nouns. A string of adjectives that describe a noun are called coordinating adjectives. These adjectives come before the noun they modify. They are separated by commas. One important thing to note, however, is that unlike listing nouns, the word “and” does not always need to be before the last adjective.

It was a bright, windy, clear day.
Our kite glowed red, yellow, and blue in the morning sunlight.

Capitalize Proper Nouns

Proper nouns are always capitalized. Proper nouns are the names of specific people, places, objects, streets, buildings, events, or titles of individuals.

Incorrect: He grew up in harlem, new york.
Correct: He grew up in Harlem, New York.

A Note on AI and This Assignment

AI tools can produce a polished professional email in seconds. For this assignment, that is the problem. The email is not the goal. The goal is the thinking behind it: analyzing your audience, selecting your appeals, and understanding why your choices work for this specific person in this specific moment.

If AI writes your email, you will reach Part 3 with nothing to analyze, because AI made the decisions without you. You cannot explain choices you did not make. Complete Parts 1 and 2 yourself before opening any AI tool. After you have a complete draft, you may use AI to check grammar and spelling using these approved prompts:

  • “Please identify grammar and spelling errors in this paragraph without changing my ideas: [paste your text]”
  • “Which sentences in this paragraph could be clearer? Please point them out without rewriting: [paste your text]”
  • “Help me express this idea more clearly in English while keeping my original meaning: [paste specific sentence]”

Why This Assignment Matters

In most professional environments, how you handle a conflict before it happens matters more than how you handle it after. A well-timed, specific advance notice protects your professional reputation, gives your supervisor room to plan, and demonstrates the kind of reliability that earns trust over time. This assignment gives you a structured opportunity to practice that skill using a situation from your own professional life.
