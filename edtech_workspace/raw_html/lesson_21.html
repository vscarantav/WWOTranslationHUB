Introduction

Things change over time. Some change quickly, like the weather or stock prices. Others change slowly, like the depth of the Grand Canyon or the body recovering from a significant injury. Change, whether fast or slow, is always happening. When making decisions using the Quantitative Reasoning Process, it is crucial to determine how quickly things will change. The need to identify the rate of change is especially true of financial decisions.

When you consider saving money or taking out loans, the rate at which your savings or loan balance changes is essential. When you save money, look at the rate at which interest builds up and adds to your balance. When you take out a loan, you want the interest you pay each month to decrease as time passes. However, since interest on loans compounds, your loan balance can grow exponentially. Loans can quickly get out of control if you do not manage your finances well.

Amadi’s Savings

1. Understand the Problem

Amadi wants to save enough money to buy a car to provide himself with transportation necessary to build his business. He needs the car soon, in no less than a year. He plans to buy a reliable pre-owned vehicle to save money. He has $1,000 saved, but he expects a used car to cost $5,000. Amadi needs to know how much to save each month. He can get an interest rate close to 1.5% APR on a car loan and buy the car immediately. Rather than get a loan, however, he would prefer to pay cash. Amadi determines how much money to save each month to reach his goal. The savings account will also pay a 1.5% APR.

2. Identify Variables & Assumptions

The variables in this situation are as follows:

  • The amount Amadi needs to save each month

  • The total amount of money saved in one year

Amadi makes the following assumptions:

  • He will be able to use the $1,000 he has already saved

  • He will be able to find a reliable pre-owned car for $5,000

The total amount of money Amadi will have saved in one year is a function of the amount he saves each month.

3. Apply Quantitative Tools

The Excel payment function PMT, found in Lesson 7, will help Amadi determine how much money he needs to save each month. In Lesson 7, you used the PMT to find loan payment amounts. The PMT function is used in a similar way to compare payments on a savings account.

The syntax for the PMT function is:

=PMT (rate, number of periods, loan amount)

For savings account deposits, the PMT function also has a fourth optional input:

=PMT (rate, number of periods, PV, [FV])

In this case, PV stands for present value, and FV stands for future value. [FV] is in square brackets to show it is optional. To figure out how much to save each month, the current balance in your savings account is the present value (PV). The total amount you need to save is the future value (FV).

Amadi uses this command to set up his Excel file.

Notice that some numbers are positive and some are negative. A negative number is used for money “leaving your wallet,” and a positive number is for funds “entering your wallet.” Since Amadi is has $1,000 in his savings account now, the money is “leaving his wallet” and will be negative. His monthly payments are negative numbers. But the amount he will have saved at the end of one year is money he will get to take out of the bank and “put back in his wallet.” He enters it in Excel as a positive number.

The Excel output from the PMT function shows Amadi needs to save $329.80 monthly to have saved $5,000 in 1 year.

4. Make an Informed Decision

After doing these calculations, Amadi realizes he needs to save at least $329.80 monthly. He carefully examines his budget. If he is careful with the money he earns, he can save $350 each month. If he cannot get the interest rate he wants or if there is an emergency and he cannot deposit the payment in full for a month or two, the extra money will help.

5. Evaluate Your Reasoning

After saving for three months, Amadi reviews and evaluates his plan. He finds that saving $350 every month is difficult. It requires him to be extremely careful about the money he spends, but he is making it work. He finds his initial assumptions to be accurate. He is happy with the progress he has made. Amadi realizes that he can continue saving this amount every month even after he purchases the car he needs and have money for other necessary capital expenses that will help grow his business.

Change Over Time

Amadi’s account balance changes over time as he earns interest and makes new deposits to the account. The speed at which things change is called the rate of change . Rates of change apply in many situations. For example, imagine you just bought a new car. The car’s value goes down the moment you drive it off the lot of the car sales business, and will continue to decrease. This is why Amadi chose to buy an older, already used vehicle. The value of a used car has already decreased. There are other costs to consider as well. The cost of gas rises and falls. As the car ages, systems wear out and vehicle maintenance costs go up.

Predicting and understanding how things change helps you use the Quantitative Reasoning Process to make good decisions when change occurs over time.

Constant and Variable Slopes

Graphs of functions provide a visual way to see how fast a quantity changes. The rate of change is represented in graphs and functions by the slope of the line or the curve. Some functions have constant slopes; some functions have variable slopes. Consider this distinction when looking at change over time.

Constant Slope

Here is an example of a function with a constant slope. Katrina earns an annual salary of $32,000 at her first job after graduation. Every year, her employer gives her a raise of $1,600. Her salary is a function of her time working at the store.

In the following equation, y represents her yearly income and x represents the number of years she has worked for the company. The resulting equation is

$$ y=1600x+32000. $$

Let’s look at some inputs and outputs of this function:

Our chart shows that the function \( y=1600x+32000 \) accurately represents Karina’s situation. The chart shows that for each additional year Karina works for the company, her salary increases by $1,600.

This linear function has a slope of \( m=1600 \) and a \( y \)-intercept of 32000. In the graph, the slope of the line represents the rate of change.

The slope is the change in \( y \) divided by the change in \( x \). In this equation, the slope is 1600.

$$ \text{slope}=\frac{\text{change in y}}{\text{change in x}}=\frac{\text{change in annual salary}}{\text{change in years worked}}=\frac{1600}{1} $$

When the number of years worked increases by one year, Karina’s annual salary changes by $1,600. Notice that this slope is consistent. Every year, Karina’s salary increases by the same amount. It always goes up by $1,600 per year. The graph shows this as a straight line. The slope is always the same. You can find another point if you go to any part of the graph, go up by 1600, then move right 1.

The slope of a function is the rate of change. The slope tells you how fast the output value changes with a variation in the input. If the graph is a straight line, the rate of change (and the slope) is constant between any two points.

Linear functions represent situations with a constant rate of change.

The raise amount is not constant. This function has a variable slope or a variable rate of change.

If a function has a constant slope, it is a linear function . If a function has a variable slope, it will be a nonlinear function .

Nonlinear functions represent situations with variable rates of change.

There are many different types of nonlinear functions. The nonlinear function in this example is exponential.

Comparing Karina’s and Adriana’s salary functions on the same graph makes it easier to compare them and see that the graph of Adriana’s salary function is not a line. At first, their salaries are similar, but after a few years, Adriana’s salary starts to pull away from Katrina’s salary. Katrina’s income continues to grow at a constant rate, but Adriana’s salary grows larger each year. This is the difference between linear and nonlinear rates of change.

Membership has grown significantly since the organization of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in 1830, which consisted of only six members. Has the change been constant or variable? One way to determine the type of change is to look at a graph of Church membership to see if the pattern is linear or nonlinear.

Understanding Interest

As part of your monthly budget, you should plan to save some money every month for an emergency or a large purchase. You can physically keep the money (for example, in an envelope or in a home safe) or, if you have access to a bank, you can put it in a savings account in your bank. The advantage of a savings account is that you earn interest on the money you save. Banks pay compound interest .

Compound interest is earned both on the original amount and on any accumulated interest.

When you earn compound interest, you are earning interest on your interest. This means your account balance can grow quickly. Putting money in an envelope every month leads to a constant rate of change . It grows only according to the amount you put in. Putting money in an account that pays compound interest leads to a variable rate of change . Not only does your account increase by the amount you put in, but it also increases as you earn interest.

Compound Interest Equations: Savings

The future value command, FV, in Excel is used to compute the account balances of savings accounts that pay compound interest.

The FV function uses six variables. These are the variable names used in Excel:

  • FV = Final Balance (future value)

  • PMT = Monthly Payment

  • PV = Principal Balance (present value)

  • r = Annual Interest Rate (written as a decimal)

  • n = number of compoundings per year

  • Y = number of years

Excel is useful because it allows you to calculate the future value of a savings account quickly and easily, as seen in the example below.

Example 1: Using Excel

Carlos wants to start a savings account. He has $2,000 he can deposit now and plans to add $50 each month. Carlos will earn a 2.0% annual interest rate that is compounded monthly. Carlos wants to know how much money he will have in 10 years.

Solution: Using Excel

Carlos could use Excel to find the answer. He is looking for the future value if his account, so he will use the FV command in Excel.

The syntax for the FV command is

$$ = FV(rate, nper, PMT, [PV]) $$

The rate Excel uses is a monthly rate, so Carlos will need to divide his annual interest rate by 12 to find the monthly rate. Excel also asks for nper . This refers to the total number of periods, or the number of payments. He will make 12 payments a year for 10 years. So he needs to multiply 10*12 to find the value of nper.

Carlos also has to rememeber to include negatives where appropriate. In Excel, PMT, and PV would be negative in a savings situation because that is money you are taking out of your wallet and putting in the bank right now. The future value of his account will be positive because it will be money he can take out of the bank and put back into his wallet.

Carlos types the following command into Excel to find his answer:

$$ = FV(0.02/12,10*12,-50,-2000) $$


At the end of 10 years, Carlos will have $9,078.38 in his account.

Non-interest vs Interest Savings Plans

How much of a difference does compound interest make? In order to see this, compare two different retirement savings situations. In the first situation, imagine that you save money each month for retirement by putting it in an envelope at home. In the second situation, imagine that you invest the money in a retirement account that earns an average of 7.5% interest annually.

In both situations, assume you just graduated from college and are beginning to save for retirement. You currently have $1,200 and will save an additional $270 each month. You plan to retire in 35 years.

The Non-interest Plan

Currently, you have $1,200 saved in an envelope at home. Every month, you plan to add $270 to the envelope. This adds up to an additional $3,240 per year. Because the account balance changes by the same amount every year, there is a constant rate of change. Use a linear function to describe the amount of money you have saved (The compound interest equations don’t apply because you are not earning any interest).

$$ f(x) = 3240x + 1200 $$

In this equation the input, \( x \), is the number of years you have been saving. The output, \( f(x) \), is the amount of money you have saved. Find out how much money you will have saved in 35 years by using an input of \( x=35 \).

$$ f(35) = 3240(35) + 1200 = $114600 $$


The Interest Savings Plan

Currently, you have $1,200 saved in a retirement account that earns an average annual interest rate of 7.5% compounded monthly. That means every month the current balance in your account is used to calculate the interest, which they add to your balance, so the next month you start earning interest on the interest you earned last month. Every month, you plan to add $270 to the account. Because the account balance changes by a different amount every month as the balance continues to grow, the rate of change is variable.

The FV function in Excel uses the following exponential function to calculate the future value of a savings account with interest:

$$ FV = PV(1 + \frac{r}{n})^{(nY)} + PMT\frac{((1 + \frac{r}{n})^{(nY)}-1)}{(\frac{r}{n})} $$
$$ f(x) = 1200(1 + \frac{0.075}{12})^{(12x)} + 270\frac{((1 + \frac{0.075}{12})^{(12x)} - 1)}{(\frac{0.075}{12})} $$

This equation looks complicated, but if you use arithmetic, it can be simplified to a simple exponential function.

Simplifying the equation yields the following:

$$ f(x)=44400(1.00625)^{12x} -43200 $$

In this equation, just like in the non-interest plan, the input, \( x \), is the number of years you have been saving. The output, \( f(x) \), is the amount of money you have saved. We can find out how much money you will have saved in 35 years by computing:

$$ f(35)=44400(1.00625)^{(12·35)}-43200=$564,736.46 $$

NOTE: If you round during the intermediate steps of the calculation above, the final answer may be off some from the error resulting by this rounding.

Comparing the Non-interest and the Interest Savings Plans

Comparing the balance of the non-interest plan and the interest plan after 35 years shows the difference compound interest can make. In both situations, you started with the $1,200 and saved $270 every month for 35 years. When you saved the money with no interest, you ended with $114,600, and when you saved the money in an account earning compound interest, you ended with $564,736.46. The difference in the account balances is due entirely to earning compound interest.

Earning Interest versus Paying Interest

It is to your advantage to earn compound interest when you are saving money. However, when you borrow money, you are charged compound interest. When you pay back a loan, compound interest works against you. Albert Einstein supposedly said, “Compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world. He who understands it, earns it… He who doesn’t, pays it” (Albert Einstein, Albert Einstein Quotes ).

The First Presidency of the Church teaches, “We encourage you wherever you may live in the world to prepare for adversity by looking to the condition of your finances. We urge you to be modest in your expenditures; discipline yourselves in your purchases to avoid debt. Pay off debt as quickly as you can, and free yourselves from this bondage. Save a little money regularly to gradually build a financial reserve” ( All Is Safely Gathered In: Family Finances pamphlet, 2007).

One reason debt becomes a source of temporal and spiritual bondage is because it accrues compound interest.
