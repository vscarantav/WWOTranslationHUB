Can you find the pattern in the list of numbers above? Humans have an amazing ability to identify patterns in images. Look at the image below. There are two tigers in the picture, hidden by the grass and camouflaged by their stripes. In spite of this, the human eye quickly locates the two tigers crouching in the grass.

Data visualization is the science of communicating important patterns from data through the use of graphics.

Using this image as an example, you could say that the goal of a graphic is to help you quickly identify the “tiger in the grass.” A good graphic should reveal important patterns in data.

Graphics can be helpful as part of the Quantitative Reasoning Process. They provide a tool for examining data that enables a more powerful decision on the part of the user. To do this, graphics must communicate the truth data has to offer in a situation of interest.

Example 1

A world map was created using data from the International Telecommunications Union to show the level of Internet penetration (number of Internet users as a percentage of a country's population).

A satelite Internet company wants to market their product on various continents. Use the following map to help determine possible markets for their product.

(image from wikimedia commons )

Solution

The countries in yellow and red would be in need of Internet connectivity. There are various options on each continent. For example, they could market their product in Papua New Guinea in the Pacific, central Africa on the African continent, Peru and Bolivia in South America, Honduras and El Salvador in Central America, and Mongolia and Laos in Asia. There are a lot of potential markets for their product. They would have to look at other data or information to narrow down the choices.

Example 1 illustrates how graphics can improve our ability to understand data. Looking at the graphic helps us get an overall impression of Internet usage throughout the world. Without a graphic, you would have to look at a list of Internet users as a percentage of a country's population. This would be a very long list of data, and without using a graphic, it would be much harder to get a good overall idea of what the data communicates.

Common Graphical Tools

There are several types of graphics that are commonly used to communicate information. Learning how and when to use these common graphical tools will benefit you as a student, as well as in the workplace.

Bar Charts

When data has several categories, the number of observations in each category can be visually summarized with a bar chart.

Example 2

Use a graphic to compare the enrollment in BYU-Pathway Worldwide between 2020 and 2023. What information does the chart provide?

Solution

A bar chart is a good graphic to use for this data because the data has three categories (winter, spring, and fall semesters) for each of the three years.

Information from an article posted on www.usu.edu

This graphic shows the growth of BYU-Pathway enrollment from 2020–2022. Fall semester has the largest enrollment numbers for all three years, and enrollment has steadily increased. The graph also shows that enrollment for Spring Semester 2022 dropped from the previous year. At the same time, between 2020 and 2021 Spring Semester enrollment increased significantly. This inconsistency, highlighted visually in the graph, leads to the question as to the possible reasons for the increase, then decline in enrollment.

Pie Charts

Pie charts are a useful alternative to bar charts. Like bar charts, pie charts can effectively display data with multiple categories. Unlike a bar chart, however, pie charts visualize the percentage of the whole “pie” or data set, that belongs to each “slice” or category, in the data.

Time Series Plots

If you look at data that has been gathered over a period of time, you can see patterns and trends. Time series plots can visualize patterns and trends like growth, decay, as well as repeating (cyclical) patterns.

Solution

A time series plot is perfect in this situation because you want to see how the number of on-campus students at BYU-Idaho has changed over time. We will use the total of part-time and full-time students that were living on campus during that semester. The data was taken from the BYU-Idaho Housing website under the Historical link.


Notice the cyclical pattern within each year. Typically the spring semester has the lowest enrollments of a given year. The overall growth of the university is also visible in the graph showing “a steady, upward course” ( A Steady, Updward Course by President Henry B. Eyring).

Scatterplots

A scatterplot is similar to a time series plot, but time is not used as one of the data elements. Scatterplots visualize the relationship between two different units.

Example 5

The US Department of Education has a useful resource called the College Scorecard to assist college-bound students in making important decisions about what college to attend. One important consideration for college students is the amount of student loan debt they may take on during college. Use the information provided by the College Scorecard to determine whether students who attend bigger universities end up with more debt.

Solution

The scatterplot below represents the data from the College Scorecard website. Notice that the horizontal axis represents the median debt of student graduates. The vertical axis represents the number of undergraduates enrolled.


This graph answers the question: Do students who attend bigger universities end up with more debt? If this were true, you would expect the data points to follow a linear pattern with large values on the horizontal axis corresponding to large values of the vertical axis. This is not the case. Visualizing the data using a scatter plot shows that there isn’t a pattern.

Since there is no pattern in the data, you can make an assumption that there is no correlation between the size of a university and the median debt load of its graduates. Note that all three Brigham Young University schools are on the lower end of the median student loan debt spectrum. However, BYU-Idaho has the highest median student loan debt of the three while being in the middle for the number of undergraduates enrolled.

Clearly there are other factors, like tuition, that would provide better explanations of student debt.

Bicycle Safety Example

Graphics are a helpful tool that can be used in Step 3 of the Quantitative Reasoning Process. Consider the following example.

1. Understand the Problem

The Ministry of Transportation in Abuja, the capital of Nigeria, would like funding from the government in order to complete a road project. Their goal is to convince the government that there is a need for a road project and that they need to provide this funding. If they get the funding, they plan to build more bicycle lanes. They believe that if there are more bicycle lanes, less people will drive cars and this will lead to reduced road fatalities. Whether or not that is correct would be the subject of a separate study. The goal of this project is to demonstrate to the government of Nigeria that there is a need for a road project.

2. Identify Variables & Assumptions

The Ministry uses a study that shows crash fatality trends in Abuja from 1990–2016.

They must make some important assumptions:

  • As the number of cyclists increases, the number of cars on the roadway will decrease.

  • The decrease in vehicles on the roadway should lead to fewer traffic accidents and fatalities.

  • Since they will be looking at Abuja and surrounding cities, they are assuming that each city reports crash fatalities in the same way and with the same accuracy.

  • The crash fatality time series data they have is not adjusted for population changes in Abuja, and thus they are assuming that it is only total crash fatalities that are important to consider, regardless of the population of the city.

To bolster their request for funding, the Ministry will try to demonstrate that there are too many crash fatalities and a road project needs to be done. The trend seen in the time series plot below clearly demonstrates a significant increase in fatalities over time.

To strengthen their argument, the Ministry presents data on crashes throughout the country of Nigeria that demonstrates that Abuja has the highest number of crash fatalities in the country. This can be seen in the second graphic below.

3. Apply Quantitative Tools

4. Make an Informed Decision

The Ministry decides the above data visuals help demonstrate that there is a need for a road improvement project in Abuja in order to reduce the number of crash fatalities. They put together a presentation with these visuals and present it to their government officials.

Because there is a pattern in this data, the Ministry is able to use the information to support the need that will be addressed with their bicycle lane initiative. While this data does not specifically support bicycle lanes as a solution to the pattern of rising accidents in Abuja, the data definitively demonstrates that there is a need for alternate modes of transportation. Future studies will show how well the bicycle lanes worked to reduce accidents.

The crucial thing to realize here is that visualizing the data makes it much easier to understand the fundamental problems. The time series plot clearly visualizes the increase in fatalities, and the use of the color bar in the Geographical Distribution graphic makes it abundantly clear visually that Abuja has the highest rate of fatalities.
