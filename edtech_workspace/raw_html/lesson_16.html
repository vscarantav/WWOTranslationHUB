Chapter 5 Writing Project

Overview

  • Task : You will select one meaningful experience from your life and craft it into a complete personal narrative. Your narrative should follow the three-part structure from Week 5 (beginning, middle, end), develop a clear central moment, and use showing rather than telling to bring the experience to life for readers. This narrative will preserve your story for future family members while demonstrating your mastery of first-person storytelling.
  • Purpose: To practice storytelling by shaping a meaningful experience into a clear, engaging narrative using effective writing techniques.
  • Length : 1000–1500 words

Instructions

Step 1: Select

Choose one significant personal experience that reveals your character, values, or growth. Look for a story that spans a brief, specific timeframe: a single day, a single conversation, or a single moment of realization. The chapter’s core lesson applies here: a story about one specific morning will be more powerful than a story that tries to cover years.

Ask yourself: What is the central moment of this story? That might be a decision you made, words someone spoke to you, a realization that struck you, or an action that changed something. If you can identify that central moment, you have found your story. If you cannot, keep looking.

Step 2: Plan

Before you write, map your narrative using the three-part structure from Week 5:

  • Beginning : Where will you drop readers into the scene? Start close to the action, not years before it. What sensory detail will appear in your first sentence?
  • Middle : What specific scenes, dialogue, and internal reactions will you show? What builds toward your central moment?
  • End : How will you reflect on what the experience meant? What specific detail from your story will you reference in your reflection, so it feels earned rather than generic?

You do not need to follow this plan exactly but having it before you write will prevent the most common problem in personal narrative: starting too early and covering too much ground.

Step 3: Draft

Write your complete narrative using first-person perspective. As you draft, apply the techniques from Week 5:

  • Show your central moment rather than summarizing it. If something important happened, let readers see it through dialogue, action, and sensory detail.
  • Choose specific details over general ones. Not “the market was loud” but the specific sound you remember.
  • Include your internal thoughts and reactions as events unfolded. This is what first-person perspective offers that no other viewpoint can.
  • Weave in your cultural context naturally, the way Ada did when she described how Nigerian values of patience and respect shaped her approach to business.

Step 4: Revise

Read your draft and identify one thing in each of these three areas:

  • One place where you are summarizing what happened instead of showing it through scene, dialogue, or sensory detail. Rewrite that section using showing techniques.
  • One place where your reflection makes a general statement rather than connecting to a specific detail from your story. Revise it to ground the insight in something concrete.
  • One place where your voice shifts to a more formal or generic tone. Read that section aloud and revise it until it sounds like you speaking.

Step 5: Requirements Checklist

Before you submit, verify:

  • Format using Times New Roman 12-point font, double-spaced, 1-inch margins.
  • Save as PDF with filename: w05_personal_narrative-FirstName_LastName.pdf

Step 6: Submit the Assignment

  • Submit your assignment to W05 Writing Project in Canvas, which is due by the end of the week.

English Writing: Mastering the Basics

Commas after an Introductory Word or Phrase

You may notice a comma that appears near the beginning of the sentence, usually after a word or phrase. This comma lets the reader know where the introductory word or phrase ends and the main sentence begins.

Without spoiling the surprise, we need to tell her to save the date.

In this sentence, “without spoiling the surprise” is an introductory phrase, and “we need to tell her to save the date” is the main sentence. Notice how they are separated by a comma.

When only an introductory word appears in the sentence, a comma also follows the introductory word.

Ironically, she already had plans for that day.

Dangling Modifiers

A dangling modifier is a word, phrase, or clause that describes something that has been left out of the sentence. When there is nothing that the word, phrase, or clause can modify, the modifier is said to dangle.

Incorrect: Riding in the sports car, the world whizzed by rapidly.
Correct: As Jane was riding in the sports car, the world whizzed by rapidly.

A Note on AI and This Assignment

Personal narrative is the assignment type where AI is most tempting and most likely to weaken your work. AI can generate sensory details, but those details will be plausible rather than yours. The specific sounds, smells, and weights that make your narrative authentic are locked inside your own memory. That specificity is exactly what makes a personal narrative worth reading.

Before using any AI tool, complete your own revision pass using the three checks in Step 4. These are the most important revisions you will make, and they require you as the source.

After completing your own revision, you may use AI to check grammar and spelling using these approved prompts:

  • “Please identify grammar and spelling errors in this paragraph without changing my ideas or personal voice: [paste your text]”
  • “Which sentences in this paragraph could be clearer without changing my meaning or personal experiences? Please point them out without rewriting: [paste your text]”
  • “Help me express this personal experience more clearly in English while keeping my original meaning and cultural context: [paste specific sentence]”

A growth accelerator for this assignment sounds like this: “Here is a scene from my narrative. Are there places where I am summarizing rather than showing the scene through specific detail, dialogue, or action? Point to specific sentences without rewriting them.” A dependence trap sounds like this: “Add sensory details to this scene to make it more vivid.”

Requirements Checklist

Before you submit, confirm you have included:

  • A narrative built around one specific experience and one clear central moment
  • A beginning that drops readers into the scene without lengthy background
  • A middle that shows the experience through specific scenes, dialogue, and sensory detail
  • An end that reflects on meaning using specific details from the story, not general statements
  • Authentic first-person voice with cultural context woven in naturally
  • Content appropriate for a family audience across generations
  • Correct formatting: Times New Roman 12-point, double-spaced, saved as PDF
Success Criteria

Excellent work will:

  • Focus on one specific experience and develop it with rich sensory detail
  • Show the central moment rather than summarizing it
  • Include a reflection that connects to specific story details rather than making general statements
  • Maintain an authentic first-person voice throughout
  • Honor the cultural and family context of the experience
Why This Assignment Matters

Personal narrative writing develops skills that transfer directly to professional life: the ability to select what matters from a large amount of experience, to communicate that selection with specific detail and clarity, and to reflect honestly on what an experience meant. Cover letters, job interviews, and professional introductions all ask you to do exactly what this assignment asks: tell one story well, connect it to a larger theme, and let your authentic voice carry the meaning.
