The Quantitative Reasoning Process

Quantitative reasoning in mathematics means using numbers to understand and solve problems. It may sound difficult, but it simply means that you utilize basic mathematical concepts such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division to analyze information and draw conclusions from the results. For example, if you want to know how much money you will have after saving $5 every week for 10 weeks, you can use quantitative reasoning to calculate the total amount.

In this course, you will learn how the data you gather through quantitative reasoning will help you make informed decisions. Here are the steps in the Quantitative Reasoning Process:

Step 1: Understand the Problem

The Quantitative Reasoning Process is used to make decisions. Start by carefully considering the situation and making sure you understand the problem and know what decision you need to make.

Step 2: Identify Key Variables and Assumptions

Identify all the key aspects of the problem. Determine what variables (unknown numbers) are important in the problem. What are the assumptions you need to make to simplify the problem?

Step 3: Apply Quantitative Tools

Quantitative tools are mathematical models such as computation, algebra, statistics, and/or graphics.

Step 4: Make an Informed Decision

Use the results of your calculations and analysis to help you make an informed decision about the situation.

Step 5: Evaluate Your Reasoning

Look back at every step of the process. Review and evaluate your reasoning. Reflect on your answer. Does your answer make sense? If not, go back and make necessary changes.

The Quantitative Reasoning Process Example

To better understand how the Quantitative Reasoning Process works, here is an example that shows the process step-by-step:

1. Understand the Problem

Maria and Lucas are married and have two little children. Lucas is working at a local bank and, at the same time, going to school to earn a certificate through BYU-Pathway so he can get a promotion or a better job to help support their growing family. Maria is supporting the family by helping to raise their young children. Maria and Lucas are trying to decide if Lucas should be taking two classes each block or one class each block.

Maria and Lucas have identified two possible plans for Lucas to complete school and need to decide which one is better for their family.

  • Option 1 : Take one class at a time. This would make it possible for Lucas to take on additional part-time work while going to school.

  • Option 2 : Take two classes at a time. This would mean Lucas could finish school faster, but it would put a larger financial burden on the family.

Maria and Lucas have already determined that they value their education. They want to follow the advice of President Dieter F. Uchtdorf: “For members of the Church, education is not merely a good idea—it’s a commandment.” (Dieter F. Uchtdorf, “Two Principles for Any Economy,” Ensign, November 2009.)

2. Identify Variables & Assumptions

Identify Key Variables

Now that you know the problem Maria and Lucas are facing, you can identify the variables they will need to consider.

Variable : An element of a situation that has the potential to vary or change in value.

To identify the key variables, ask yourself which elements of the situation have the potential to vary or change in value. Here are some variables that Maria and Lucas should consider:

  • Tuition costs each term . If Lucas takes two classes, the tuition will be higher.

  • Lucas’s available time . The amount of time Lucas has to put toward his classes depends on his job(s) and his responsibilities at home and at church.

  • Wages from part-time employment . If Lucas gets a second job, that could help the family.

All of these variables are important for Maria and Lucas to consider when making their decision. Maria and Lucas will have to decide which variables are the most important.

Making Assumptions

In order to simplify any situation and make it more manageable, you must make some assumptions.

Assumption : An idea or concept that is accepted as true.

In the Quantitative Reasoning Process, assumptions and variables go together. By making specific, realistic assumptions about variables, you can simplify a complex situation and focus on other important aspects of the problem at hand. These assumptions remove the need for constant "what if" questions, allowing you to streamline your calculations and concentrate on critical issues.

Consider Maria and Lucas. They ask themselves, "What if tuition costs more next year?" To move forward, they must make an assumption. In our example, they assume that tuition prices will remain constant until Lucas finishes school.

They also wonder, “What if living expenses cost more next year?” Again, an assumption is required to continue. Maria and Lucas make these assumptions:

  • Their living expenses will remain fairly constant for the time Lucas needs to finish school.

  • Tuition per credit costs will stay the same until Lucas graduates.

By making these assumptions, Maria and Lucas simplify their financial planning process and gain a clearer understanding of their situation. Keep in mind, if their circumstances change, they will need to re-evaluate their assumptions in the future.

Are these assumptions appropriate or not?

There are other assumptions that Maria and Lucas have to make. Read the assumptions below and decide if they are appropriate for this situation or not.

Assumption: Lucas's wages from his job will stay the same.

This is an appropriate assumption.

Assumption: Lucas will be able to get a lucrative job in a big city when he graduates.

This is not an appropriate assumption.

Assumption : Lucas will be able to get a better job or a promotion after he graduates.

This is an appropriate assumption.

Assumption: Maria and Lucas need semiannual vacations.

This is not an appropriate assumption.

It's crucial to know the appropriate assumptions because if they're incorrect, Maria and Lucas might make the wrong decision about how many classes to take. Wrong assumptions can lead to bad decisions, but it's still important to make assumptions when making decisions.

3. Apply Quantitative Tools

After identifying key variables and key assumptions, use tools like algebra, statistics, and graphs to give you data that will help you decide what to do. You will learn to use mathematical tools that will help make good decisions throughout this semester.

Estimating School and Living Expenses

Maria and Lucas have been tracking their expenses since Lucas completed PC 101. Maria and Lucas estimate they can live off of between R$1,000–1,500 a month. (Brazilian currency is the real, represented here as R$.)

Lucas’s current job pays him R$1,250 a month. Another part-time job would pay between R$300–400 a month.

Tuition costs R$96 per credit hour. If Lucas takes one class each block, it will cost him about R$288 every three months. If he takes two classes each block, it will cost twice that much.

Calculate the cost of tuition by month:

288 cost for one class for three months / 3 = 96 cost for one class for one month

96 cost for one class for one month * 2 = 192 cost for two classes for one month

4. Make an Informed Decision

Now that Maria and Lucas have modeled the situation, they need to interpret the information they have gathered.

  • Option 1: Take one class at a time. If Lucas takes one class at a time, they will have more money to live off of, but Lucas will be working a second job so he will have to adjust where he’s spending his time to make it work.

  • Option 2: Take two classes at a time. If Lucas takes two classes the finances will be tight, and they will have to adjust their time expectations a little to make things work.

5. Evaluate Your Reasoning

Once Maria and Lucas have made a decision, it is important that they reflect on and evaluate their decision. They should ask themselves questions like these:

  • Does this decision make sense?

  • Are my assumptions reasonable?

  • Are there any consequences of this decision I haven’t considered?

  • What types of events could happen that would violate my assumptions? (Perhaps an unplanned illness or the loss of a job). Do I have a plan to deal with these events?

  • Have I prayed about the decision?

Conclusion

The Quantitative Reasoning Process is a way to carefully consider difficult choices and to make informed decisions.

The situation presented in this lesson is just one example of how the Quantitative Reasoning Process can help you make informed decisions. During this semester as you learn to make informed decisions in all aspects of your life, you will be more prepared to serve others in your home, church, and community.
