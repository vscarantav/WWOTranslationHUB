Opening Case Study: Bringing Oral Tradition and Written Documentation

Ada Okonkwo, a BYU-Idaho Pathway student from Lagos who studies while working full-time, faced a challenging research assignment. She needed to write a paper about small business practices in Nigeria. She had extensive knowledge from watching her mother run a successful fabric shop for twenty years. She had learned business practices from aunts, uncles, and family friends who operated various enterprises. Yet her instructor’s feedback indicated she needed “more documented sources.”

“In my community, knowledge passes from one generation to the next through observation and oral teaching,” Ada explains. “My mother never read a business textbook, yet she built a thriving business and trained many apprentices. When I wrote about these proven practices in my research paper, my instructor said I needed to find ‘published sources’ to support what I knew from experience.”

Ada initially felt frustrated. “I wanted to cite ‘my mother’ or ‘what everyone in Lagos knows about business.’ The practices I described are real and effective. Why did I need to find an American professor who wrote about them in a journal?”

Working with a tutor, Ada began to understand Western academic expectations for written documentation. She learned to search for scholarly articles about informal economics, apprenticeship systems, and small business practices in West Africa. She discovered that some researchers had indeed documented the practices she knew from experience.

“I found a fascinating study that described exactly how my mother’s apprenticeship system works,” Ada says. “The researcher called it ‘informal skills transfer’ and analyzed its effectiveness. Reading academic language about something I had seen my whole life felt strange but also validating.”

For her final paper, Ada combined personal observation with scholarly sources. “I wrote: ‘According to Meagher (2010), Nigerian market traders develop sophisticated inventory management systems through apprenticeship practices. I observed this approach in my mother’s fabric business, where apprentices learned to...’ This way, I could honor both the oral knowledge from my community and the academic requirement for documented sources.”

Ada’s experience demonstrates the challenge many international students face. They must bridge oral tradition knowledge systems with Western academic documentation requirements while respecting both approaches.

Ada’s experience raises a question worth considering before you begin your own research: could AI help bridge this same gap? In some ways, yes. You could ask an AI tool to explain how Western academic documentation works, or to suggest search terms for finding published research on practices you already know from your community. These are examples of using AI as a growth accelerator. They help you think and search more effectively without replacing the intellectual work of evaluating sources yourself. Falling into the dependence trap looks different. It means asking AI to find and summarize your sources for you, then citing those summaries as though you had read the originals. This approach removes the very skills this chapter is designed to build. It also creates a serious integrity risk, because AI regularly produces citations that look accurate but cannot be verified in any database. Whatever tools you use, the thinking, reading, and documentation decisions belong to you.

Lesson 4.1: The Research Writing Process

In this section, you will learn how research writing follows a systematic six-step process, how each step builds upon the previous one, and how to manage research projects effectively from start to finish.

How does a research paper grow from brainstormed notes to a polished final draft? No two projects are identical, but most follow the same six basic steps. You have already practiced the writing process in Week 1 and considered purpose and audience in Week 3. Research writing builds on those same skills while adding a more systematic approach to planning and integrating sources. All you need to get started is a general topic , access to research materials, and enough time to work through each stage thoughtfully.

The six steps of the research writing process are:

  1. Choose a topic.
  2. Plan and schedule time to research and write.
  3. Conduct research.
  4. Organize your research and ideas.
  5. Draft your paper.
  6. Revise and edit your paper.

The six steps for research writing are process tasks. Its purpose is to develop your ability to think, research, and write independently. AI works best on Steps 2, 4, and 6, where you can use it to brainstorm a research schedule, identify whether your organized ideas have any gaps, or to check your draft for clarity after you have already made your own judgments. AI is least appropriate at Steps 1, 3, and 5. At those steps of core intellectual work, choosing your focus, evaluating sources, and drafting your own analysis, is exactly the skill this course is training you to develop. A growth accelerator prompt at Step 4 might sound like this: “Here is my working thesis and the main points I have organized. What questions are left unanswered?” A dependence trap prompt at Step 5 sounds like this: “Write a research paper about Nigerian apprenticeship systems using these sources.” The first prompt helps you think. The second prompt thinks for you.

Step 1: Choosing a Topic

Choosing a topic is more than picking a subject you find interesting. A good research topic needs to be focused enough to explore thoroughly within the scope of your paper, yet broad enough to have sufficient sources available. The goal of this step is to move from a general area of interest to a specific, researchable question.

Once you’ve identified a general area of interest, the next challenge is transforming that broad subject into a manageable research focus.

To narrow the focus of your topic, try freewriting exercises such as brainstorming to help you explore your ideas. From there, you will want to develop a specific research question , a broad, open-ended question that will guide your research. You’ll then determine your working thesis, which is your proposed answer to that question.

Once you have both, you can bring them together in a research proposal. This document presents your main research question, any related sub-questions you plan to explore, and your working thesis , giving you a clear roadmap before you begin researching in earnest.

Practice Example: Developing a Research Question and Working Thesis

Topic Area: Community gardens

Initial Research Question: How do community gardens affect neighborhoods?

Refined Research Question: How do community gardens impact food security and community cohesion in low-income neighborhoods?

Working Thesis: Community gardens provide multiple benefits to low-income neighborhoods. They improve access to fresh produce, create opportunities for social interaction across cultural divides, and transform unused spaces into productive community assets.

By refining the research question and developing a working thesis, the writer creates a focused direction while remaining open to adjusting their thesis as new information emerges through research.

Step 2: Planning and Scheduling

Before you start researching your topic, take time to plan your research and writing schedule thoughtfully. Research projects can take days, weeks, or even months to complete. Creating a realistic schedule is one of the best ways to ensure you do not end up feeling overwhelmed. All the work can seem manageable when you break it down into smaller tasks.

During this step, it is also helpful to plan the resources you will use. Think about the organizational tools that will keep you on track throughout the project. Consider using a simple calendar to mark important deadlines, a notebook or folder to keep your sources organized, and a checklist to track which tasks you've completed. Good planning at this stage will save you considerable stress later.

Step 3: Conducting Research

With your plan in place, you are ready to begin researching. During this phase, you will draw from a rich variety of sources, including books, periodicals, video presentations, and in-person interviews, each offering unique insights and perspectives.

Your sources will include both primary sources and secondary sources . Primary sources provide firsthand information or raw data, such as surveys, interviews, and historical documents that come directly from the time or event. Secondary sources include some analysis or interpretation of primary materials, such as biographies, literary reviews, or magazine articles written about events or works.

As you conduct research, you will take detailed, careful notes about your discoveries that will become the foundation of your paper. Not all sources are equally trustworthy or appropriate for academic research, so you will need to carefully evaluate the reliability of each source you find.

Step 4: Organizing Research and Ideas

When your research is complete, you will organize your findings by deciding which sources to cite and evaluating whether the evidence you collected supports your thesis. If it doesn’t, conduct additional research and adjust your thesis to ensure it is well supported.

Step 5: Drafting Your Paper

Now you are ready to combine your research findings with your own critical analysis into a rough draft, incorporating source materials into your paper and determining which ones relate best to your thesis.

When you cite your reference sources, pay close attention to standard conventions to avoid plagiarism, the practice of using someone else’s words without acknowledging the source. Later in this chapter, you will learn how to incorporate sources correctly and avoid the most common pitfalls.

Step 6: Revising and Editing Your Paper

In the final step, you will revise and polish your paper by examining its overall structure, unity, and cohesion to ensure that each element in your paper flows logically and naturally into the next, while also making sure the tone remains appropriate and consistent throughout.

Once you feel confident in the strength of your writing, you will edit your paper, checking for proper spelling, grammar, punctuation, mechanics, and formatting. When you complete this final step, you will have achieved something significant: transforming a simple idea or question into a thoroughly researched paper.

Lesson 4.2: Gathering and Evaluating Sources

In this section, you will learn to distinguish between primary and secondary sources across different cultural contexts. You will also discover strategies for finding sources and evaluating their reliability. The research gathering phase corresponds to Step 3 in our 6-step process, which builds upon the planning and topic development you completed in Steps 1 and 2.

Using Primary and Secondary Sources

Writers classify research resources into two categories: primary sources and secondary sources. Primary sources are direct, firsthand sources of information or data.

Other primary sources include the following:

  • Research articles.
  • Literary texts.
  • Historical documents such as diaries or letters.
  • Autobiographies or other personal accounts.

Secondary sources discuss, interpret, analyze, consolidate, or otherwise rework information from primary sources. These sources are one step removed from the primary source of information.

The following are examples of secondary sources:

  • Magazine articles.
  • Biographical books.
  • Literary and scientific reviews.
  • Television documentaries.

Ask yourself which sources are most likely to provide the information that will answer your research questions. Once you have thought about what kinds of sources will help you, begin your search by looking at print and electronic resources. Let’s look at strategies that will help you search efficiently for the sources you need.

Be Persistent

From modern prophets and the scriptures, we learn about the importance of persistence. Elder Dieter F. Uchtdorf (2007), declared: “Enduring to the end, or remaining faithful to the laws and ordinances of the gospel of Jesus Christ throughout our life, is a fundamental requirement for salvation in the kingdom of God. This belief distinguishes Latter-day Saints from many other Christian denominations that teach that salvation is given to all who simply believe and confess that Jesus is the Christ. The Lord clearly declared, ‘If you keep my commandments and endure to the end you shall have eternal life, which gift is the greatest of all the gifts of God’ (D&C 14:7).” Keeping the end goal in mind can help make the enduring to the end easier.

Common Misconception: All Sources Are Equally Reliable

Many students assume that primary sources are always more reliable than secondary sources. This is not necessarily true. A poorly conducted survey (primary source) may be less reliable than a peer-reviewed analysis of multiple studies (secondary source). The key is evaluating each source based on its methodology, author credentials, and publication standards rather than its category alone.

Primary and Secondary Sources Across Cultures

Other cultures may have differing priorities when it comes to researching sources. As Ada discovered in our opening case study, oral tradition and experiential knowledge hold deep value in many cultures. Yet Western academic writing requires documented sources. Understanding these different approaches helps you bridge knowledge systems while maintaining academic integrity.

Japanese Research Traditions: In Japanese academic writing, secondary sources from respected authorities often carry significant weight. The hierarchical approach in this tradition values interpretations from well-known scholars, sometimes even above direct engagement with primary sources. The collective knowledge framework builds carefully upon established scholarly consensus rather than challenging existing authorities.

African Oral History Traditions: Many African academic contexts value oral histories and testimonies as important primary sources. The hierarchical approach in these traditions deeply respects elder testimonies and traditional knowledge holders as authoritative sources. The collective knowledge framework meaningfully integrates community stories and “Ngano” (storytelling) alongside written records, creating a rich tapestry of documentation that honors multiple forms of knowledge.

Western Academic Approach: Western research traditions typically prioritize primary sources and original analysis above secondary interpretations. The approach expects researchers to engage directly with primary materials and develop independent interpretations. Rather than relying heavily on established authorities, the collective knowledge framework emphasizes individual contribution and original thinking as marks of scholarly achievement.

Latin American Research Methods: Many Latin American research traditions value “testimonio” (personal testimony) as legitimate primary documentation. The approach in these contexts often elevates voices from marginalized individuals and communities as important sources. The collective knowledge framework integrates community narratives with more traditional academic sources in ways that honor diverse forms of knowledge and experience.

Despite these differences, all of these research traditions share important common ground, including a commitment to credible sources and careful documentation. At the same time, they differ in how they approach authority, the role of individual analysis, and the importance placed on different types of sources. Recognizing both the similarities and the differences can help you communicate more effectively in global academic settings and adapt your research strategies when working across cultures.

Using Internet Search Engines Efficiently

The next important principle is understanding the limitations of popular search engines. When faced with research paper challenges, some students rely on popular search engines as their first source of information.

Typing a keyword or phrase into a search engine instantly creates links. You might see dozens, hundreds, or even thousands of related websites. This seems convenient. Unfortunately, this research strategy has several important drawbacks you should consider.

Results do not always appear in order of reliability. In fact, the first few hits may include sites whose content is not particularly reliable. Online encyclopedias can be edited by any user. This means search engines cannot always tell you which sites have accurate information. Additionally, results may be too numerous for you to use effectively. The amount of information available on the web is far greater than what you would find in a particular library or database. If your web search creates thousands of hits, you simply cannot visit every site. The most useful sites may be buried deep within your search results. Finally, search engines are not connected to the results they provide.

Search engines locate websites that people visit often and list results based on popularity rather than accuracy. When you cite a source found through a search engine, you do not need to cite the search engine itself. Only cite the original source.

Common Misconception: First Search Results Are Most Reliable

Many students believe that the first few results from a search engine are the most trustworthy sources. A well-designed but inaccurate website may appear before a credible academic source. Always evaluate each source on its own merits rather than assuming search result order reflects quality.

Strategies for Evaluating Online Sources

When using internet sources, follow these steps to evaluate reliability:

  1. Check the author’s credentials
    • Is the author an expert in the field?
    • Are they affiliated with a respected institution?
    • Can you find other published works by this author?
  2. Examine the website domain
    • Educational (.edu), governmental (.gov), and organizational (.org) sites often contain more reliable information than commercial (.com) sites.
    • Check if the site represents an established institution.
  3. Look for recent updates
    • When was the information last updated?
    • Is the information current for your topic?
  4. Assess objectivity
    • Does the site show bias or present multiple perspectives?
    • Is the purpose to inform or to persuade/sell?
    • Are claims supported by evidence?
  5. Verify information across sources
    • Can you confirm key information in other reliable sources?
    • Do other experts in the field support these findings?

Using these strategies will help you identify trustworthy online sources for your research.

  • Domain: www.climateresearchnow.org
  • Last updated: 6 months ago

Sample Response: Need to verify Dr. Chen’s institutional affiliation, check for peer review of her work, confirm chart data sources, and cross-reference key claims with other reliable sources.

These source evaluation skills will be essential for your personal history project, where you may need to verify family stories and historical claims using online resources.

One tool you may already be using to find sources is generative AI. AI tools can suggest relevant search terms, describe the types of sources that typically address a topic, and provide a broad overview of what a field of research covers. These are legitimate starting points. What AI cannot do reliably is give you accurate citations . AI regularly produces bibliographic references, complete with author names, journal titles, volume numbers, and page ranges, that do not exist in any database.

This specific type of error is called a hallucination . It is particularly dangerous in research writing because fabricated citations look identical to accurate ones. The only way to catch them is to locate every source independently in a library database or official publication before you cite it. Apply the same five-step evaluation process you just practiced to any source AI suggests. Verify the author’s credentials, check the domain or publisher, confirm the publication date, assess objectivity, and cross-reference key claims. AI can help you search. It cannot do the evaluating for you.

Library Databases and Electronic Collections

Moving from general internet searches, we examine more reliable research tools. Many schools, libraries, and communities provide access to electronic collections. These include indexed newspapers, journals, films, books, and other materials. You can use these for free or for a small fee.

This kind of database allows you to search and read academic content. You use a search engine that you can access from home or a library. These databases help locate credible research.

Interviews

With so many print and electronic media available, it is easy to overlook another valuable resource: other people. Consider whether you could use a person or group as a primary source . You might interview a professor who has expertise in a particular subject. You could interview a worker within a particular industry. You might speak with a representative from a political organization. Interviews can be a great way to get firsthand information.

Common Misconception: Interview Questions Should Yield Yes-or-No Answers

Many beginning researchers believe that clear, direct questions with yes-or-no answers are most effective for interviews. However, open-ended questions that cannot be answered with a simple yes or no are more likely to lead to detailed, useful responses. Instead of asking “Do you think technology helps students?” ask “How has technology changed the way students learn in your classroom?” This approach generates richer, more detailed information for your research.

Lesson 4.3: Using Research for Summarizing, Paraphrasing, and Quoting

Using research in your paper falls under three categories; summary notes, paraphrased information, and direct quotations from your sources. In this section, you will learn when each method is most appropriate and practice applying these techniques effectively.

This corresponds to Step 4 (organizing research) and Step 5 (drafting) in our 6-step process. You are moving from collecting sources to using them in your writing.

Summarizing Sources

When you summarize material from a source, you focus on the main points by restating them concisely in your own words. This technique works well when only the major ideas are relevant to your paper, or when complex information needs to be distilled into a few key points for your readers.

When summarizing, keep the source material in front of you and begin identifying the main idea and restate it in your own words as concisely as possible, preferably in one sentence. Depending on your purpose, you may add another sentence or two, condensing any important details or examples. Then check your summary to make sure it is accurate and complete.

Practice Example: Summarizing a Source

Original: “Climate change hits farming differently depending on where you are. In hot, tropical places, even a small rise in temperature can cut rice and corn harvests by 10-15%. Some cooler areas might do better at first because they get longer growing seasons. But as temperatures keep climbing, even these benefits start to disappear. Rain patterns are also becoming unpredictable, with some places dealing with serious droughts while others get hit with floods that destroy crops. All these differences make it hard to find solutions that work everywhere.”

Summary: “Climate change affects farms in different ways around the world. Hot regions are seeing smaller harvests, while some cooler places might benefit from longer growing seasons, at least for now. Unpredictable weather makes it tough for farmers to plan. Because every region faces different problems, there’s no one-size-fits-all solution.”

The summary captures the key points of the original passage in about one-third of the length, focusing on main ideas while omitting specific statistics and details.

Paraphrasing Sources

Paraphrasing is the process of restating information from a source using your own words and original sentence structure. The purpose is to demonstrate your understanding while maintaining the original meaning. It also helps you maintain academic integrity while demonstrating your understanding.

Effective paraphrasing involves several important elements working together. It uses original language and sentence structure rather than just replacing words with synonyms, maintaining the same level of detail as the original passage. It demonstrates your understanding of the concept rather than just mechanical word substitution. Just as important, paraphrasing still requires proper citation even though you have changed the words.

Common Misconception: Citations Are Not Needed for Paraphrased Material

Some students believe that changing the words in a source eliminates the need for citation. This is incorrect. Even when you paraphrase or summarize information from a source, you must still provide proper citation. The ideas, data, or arguments belong to the original author, regardless of how you express them in your own words. Failure to cite paraphrased material constitutes plagiarism.

Common Misconception: Paraphrasing is Just Changing a Few Words

Many students believe that paraphrasing simply means replacing key words with synonyms. They maintain the original sentence structure. This approach is sometimes called the “thesaurus method.” It is actually a form of plagiarism. It doesn’t represent original thinking.

Original text: “Exercise has been shown to reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety by triggering the release of endorphins in the brain.”

Incorrect paraphrase: “Physical activity has been demonstrated to decrease indications of depression and anxiety by initiating the discharge of endorphins in the mind.”

Correct paraphrase: “Research indicates that when people engage in physical activity, their brains produce endorphins. These chemicals can help alleviate feelings of depression and reduce anxiety symptoms.”

The correct paraphrase completely restructures the sentence by using different phrasing patterns. It demonstrates understanding of the concept rather than just swapping out words.

Paraphrasing is one of the tasks where AI presents the clearest temptation and the clearest risk. You could paste a source passage into an AI tool and ask it to paraphrase the text for you. The AI will produce a version that reads smoothly and avoids obvious word-for-word repetition. The problem is that this is not your paraphrase. It is AI’s and submitting it as your own work misrepresents both your understanding and your authorship. The AI’s version may also subtly distort the original meaning in ways you would not catch unless you had already read the source carefully yourself.

A responsible and useful approach is to write your own paraphrase first, then ask AI: “Here is my paraphrase of this passage. Does my version accurately reflect the original meaning, or have I changed anything important?” This keeps you in control of the intellectual work and uses AI to check your thinking rather than replace it. Your instructor is evaluating whether you understand your sources. That understanding cannot be outsourced.

Quoting Sources Directly

Most of the time, you will summarize or paraphrase source material instead of quoting directly. Doing so shows that you understand your research well and can write about it confidently in your own words. However, direct quotes can be powerful when used sparingly and with purpose.

Quoting directly can sometimes help you make a point in a colorful way. If an author’s words are especially vivid, memorable, or well phrased, quoting them may help hold your reader’s interest. Direct quotations from an interviewee or an eyewitness may help you personalize an issue for readers.

When you analyze primary sources, such as a historical speech or a work of literature, quoting extensively is often necessary. This helps illustrate your points. These are valid reasons to use quotations.

Less experienced writers, however, sometimes overuse direct quotations in research papers. They think it seems easier than paraphrasing. At best, this reduces the effectiveness of the quotations. At worst, it results in a paper that seems haphazardly pasted together from outside sources. Use quotations sparingly for greater impact.

When you do choose to quote directly from a source, follow these guidelines:

Make sure you have transcribed the original statement accurately. Represent the author’s ideas honestly. Quote enough of the original text to reflect the author’s point accurately. Never use a stand-alone quotation. Always integrate the quoted material into your own sentence. Use ellipses (. . .) if you need to omit a word or phrase. Use brackets [ ] if you need to replace a word or phrase. Make sure any omissions or changed words do not alter the meaning of the original text. Omit or replace words only when absolutely necessary. Do this to shorten the text or to make it grammatically correct within your sentence. Remember to include correctly formatted citations that follow the assigned style guide.

Using Primary Sources Effectively

Some types of research papers must use primary sources extensively to achieve their purpose. Any paper that analyzes primary text fall in this category. Any paper that presents the writer’s own experimental research also falls here.

Here are a few examples:

  • A paper for a literature course analyzing several poems by Emily Dickinson.
  • A paper for a political science course comparing televised speeches delivered by two presidential candidates.
  • A paper for a communications course discussing gender bias in television commercials.
  • A paper for a business administration course that discusses survey results. The writer conducted the survey with local businesses gathering information about work-from-home and flextime policies.
  • A paper for an elementary education course that discusses experiment results. The writer conducted the experiment to compare the effectiveness of 2 different methods of mathematics instruction.

For these types of papers, primary research is the main focus. This means engaging directly with the original material through close observation and careful note-taking. If you are writing about a work (including non-print works, such as a movie or a painting), you should watch the film multiple times to catch important details, examine the painting's composition and use of color, or read the novel carefully while annotating significant passages and tracking themes. Do not rely solely on others' interpretations. While scholarly articles or reviews can provide helpful context, they often function as secondary sources in this type of assignment and should not replace your own analysis. Your paper should be grounded in your careful examination of the work and your original insights.

If you take time to design and conduct your own field research, you will want to discuss it in detail. Your research might include a survey, a series of interviews, or an experiment. The interviews may provide interesting responses that you want to share with your reader.

Using Secondary Sources Effectively

For some assignments, it makes sense to rely more on secondary sources than primary sources. If you are not analyzing a text or conducting your own field research, you will need to use secondary sources extensively.

As much as possible, use secondary sources that are closely linked to primary research. Examples include a journal article presenting the results of the authors’ scientific study. A book that cites interviews and case studies is another example. These sources are more reliable. They add more value to your paper than sources that are further removed from primary research.

For instance, consider a popular magazine article on junk food addiction. It might be several steps removed from the original scientific study. It might be loosely based on that study. As a result, the article may distort, sensationalize, or misinterpret the scientists’ findings.

Even if your paper is largely based on primary sources, secondary sources can still be a valuable resource in developing your ideas. For instance, an analysis of Alfred Hitchcock’s films would focus on the films themselves as a primary source, while also drawing on commentary from critics to support your argument. Similarly, a paper presenting an original experiment would include discussion of comparable research in the field.

Lesson 4.4: Interview Write-Ups

In this section, you will learn how to transform interview conversations into polished written research. You will discover techniques for note-taking, organizing responses, integrating quotes and paraphrases, and creating comprehensive interview write-ups that meet academic standards.

Building on the interview skills you learned earlier in this chapter, we now examine how to write up interview results effectively. Many research projects require you to conduct interviews and present your findings in written form.

This builds upon the interview preparation strategies and source integration methods (summarizing, paraphrasing, and quoting) you have already learned in this chapter.

Taking Effective Interview Notes

To get the most out of an interview, you will need to take careful notes. Your note-taking strategy directly affects the quality of your write-up.

Effective note-taking involves several important practices. As your subject speaks, record key quotes exactly as spoken. Be sure to use quotation marks in your notes so you can identify these later.

Capture the exact words for any statements you might want to quote directly in your write-up. For longer responses, paraphrase them in your own words while clearly noting who said what. Write down the question you asked before recording each response. This helps you organize your notes later. Mark particularly interesting or surprising responses with a star or other symbol. When relevant, note non-verbal information such as tone, emotion, or emphasis. These details can enrich your final write-up. As soon as possible after the interview, review and clarify your notes while the conversation is still fresh in your memory. Finally, organize your notes by theme or question to prepare for writing.

Organizing Interview Responses Before Writing

Before you begin writing, take time to organize your interview material.

Start by reading through all your notes completely to refresh your memory of the entire conversation. As you read, identify main themes or patterns that emerge in your subject’s responses. Look for ideas that appear multiple times or connect to each other. Next, group related responses together by theme rather than keeping them in chronological order. This thematic organization will help you create a coherent, well-structured write-up.

Now select the most significant quotes that deserve to be presented in your subject’s own words. Choose quotes that are particularly memorable, insightful, or revealing. Identify other responses that need paraphrasing rather than direct quotation. Determine which information requires summarization to provide context without excessive detail. Finally, create an outline showing how you will present this information in your write-up. This outline will guide your writing and ensure a logical flow.

The interview write-up is a personal document. It reflects what you heard, what you noticed, and what you found meaningful in a conversation you conducted yourself. Because of this, it is a process task. The words that appear in your write-up should come from your notes and your own understanding of what your subject shared, not from an AI tool generating narrative from your bullet points. Asking AI to draft your write-up from your notes shifts the most important work, interpreting your subject’s experience and expressing it in your own voice, to a system that was not present for the conversation and cannot understand its significance.

A growth accelerator prompt after you have written your own draft might be: “Here is a paragraph from my interview write-up. Does the direct quote feel smoothly integrated, or does it read like it is standing alone?” A dependence trap prompt looks like this: “Here are my interview notes. Write a 300-word interview write-up.” One builds your ability to tell someone else’s story with honesty and care. The other produces output you cannot fully claim as yours.

Interview Write-Up Structure

An interview write-up is a document that presents information gathered from an interview in an organized, readable format for sharing insights with your readers that helps them understand what you learned.

Interview write-ups are a specialized form of research writing that combines the engaging style of narrative storytelling with proper source integration and documentation.

Done effectively, these documents present interview findings in paragraph form rather than question-and-answer format. They integrate direct quotes smoothly into complete sentences and paraphrase responses using your own words. They summarize overall findings and highlight key insights while maintaining a clear focus on what you learned from the interview.

Generally, the format includes an introduction that establishes the interview purpose and introduces your subject. Then, body paragraphs organize content by theme or question rather than following the interview chronologically. Your content will include direct quotes that includes proper attribution, so readers know who said what and paraphrased responses that show your understanding of what your subject shared. A concluding summary then presents the key insights you gained from the conversation.

These skills are essential for success interviews, oral history projects, professional research, and journalism.

Integrating Quotes and Paraphrases

Never present quotes as stand-alone sentences sitting by themselves. Always weave the quoted material into your own complete sentences. This creates a more professional and readable write-up.

Here are 3 effective techniques for integrating quotes into your writing:

Technique 1: Introduce With Attribution: When you begin with an attribution, present the quote, then add your analysis or interpretation, using this format: According to [insert name here], “direct quote here,” then your thoughts here.

Example: According to Maria Rodriguez, “the most important quality in leadership is the ability to listen,” which reflects her emphasis on collaborative decision-making.

Technique 2: Mid-Sentence Integration Start with your analysis, then embed the quote naturally within your sentence, like this: Start with your analysis, then the name of the source, followed by their direct quote, finishing with any context information.

Example: Her commitment to education became clear when Maria Rodriguez explained that “every child deserves access to quality learning opportunities” during our discussion of community programs.

Technique 3: Paraphrase with a Supporting Quote First, paraphrase the main point, then support it with a memorable direct quote, using this format: [Name of source] then your paraphrase of the main point, ending with a “direct quote here.”

Example: Rodriguez emphasized the importance of mentorship in her career, stating, “I would not be where I am today without the guidance of my early mentors.”

Common Misconception: Interview Write-Ups Should Include Every Question and Answer

Many students believe they must include every question and response in their interview write-up. This is a natural assumption. However, this approach creates a rigid, interview-transcript format rather than the flowing narrative you want to achieve.

Instead, think of yourself as a storyteller sharing meaningful insights from your conversation. Select the most significant and relevant information from your interview and then organize this information thematically around key ideas. Present it in coherent paragraphs that reads naturally. Focus on what you learned and the most important insights your subject shared. Your goal is not to reproduce the entire conversation, but to capture its essence and meaning.

When I asked James Chen about his motivation, his answer revealed a deep commitment to community service. Chen is the owner of Chen’s Hardware. He has run the business for 15 years.

Chen explained that he wanted to create a place where neighbors could find quality products. Each job represents a family he helps support. The challenges he has faced taught him valuable lessons. He learned about perseverance and adaptation. When online retailers began affecting his sales, Chen had to rethink his business model entirely. He developed workshops and community events. These transformed his store into a gathering place, not just a retail space.

The most striking insight came when I asked about his advice for aspiring entrepreneurs. Chen emphasized that “success is not measured in dollars alone but in the positive impact you have on people’s lives.” He has achieved his goal of creating sustainable employment in the community. He maintains the flexibility to spend time with his family. Currently, Chen is working toward opening a second location. Conducting Family Interviews

Process Explanation: Conducting Family Interviews

Purpose: To gather firsthand accounts, stories, and information about family history from living relatives

Required Resources: Willing family member, prepared questions about specific family events or people, recording permission, comfortable interview setting

Sequential Steps:

  1. Contact your family member early in the research process.
  2. Explain your purpose for requesting an interview.
  3. Schedule a time when they can speak without distractions.
  4. Prepare detailed questions about people, places, dates, and stories.
  5. Bring a recording device (only if you have obtained their permission) or note-taking materials.
  6. Start with easy, comfortable questions. Ask about sensitive topics later.
  7. Ask follow-up questions to clarify names, dates, and locations.
  8. Request permission to include their information in your written project.
  9. Thank them for their time and contribution.
  10. Review and organize notes immediately after the interview.
  11. Follow up with a thank-you message and any clarifying questions.

Working with Incomplete or Missing Information

Family records are often incomplete. Not every birth date or location will be available.

Strategies for Incomplete Information:

When information is partially known:

  • Include what you know and note what is missing.
  • Use approximate dates when exact dates are unavailable, such as circa 1950 or early 1960s.
  • Indicate uncertainty clearly using phrases like “believed to be,” “approximately,” or “around.”

Examples:

  • “My grandfather was born around 1945 in rural Mexico. The exact location is uncertain.”
  • “My maternal grandmother’s birth date is unknown. Family records indicate she was born in the early 1920s.”

When information is completely unavailable:

  • Clearly state that information is not currently known.
  • Explain why it might be missing if relevant.
  • Express intention to research further where possible.

Examples:

  • “My great-grandfather’s parents’ names are not currently known to our family.”
  • “Birth records from this time period in that region were not systematically maintained. Exact dates remain uncertain.”

Common Misconception: Family History Projects Must Include Complete Information

Many students worry that missing information means they cannot complete their family history project successfully. This concern is understandable, but unnecessary. Academic and personal history writing regularly acknowledges missing information as a normal part of research.

As a researcher, honestly note gaps in your knowledge. When details are unavailable, or uncertain, acknowledging what you do not know demonstrates intellectual honesty and good research practice. This approach actually strengthens rather than weakens your work.

Privacy and Ethical Considerations

When writing about living relatives, you must respect privacy and handle sensitive information appropriately. You are never required to share information that feels too personal. Where exact dates or locations are unavailable or sensitive, you may use approximate timeframes (e.g., ‘around 1970’) or simply note that the information is not currently known.

Privacy Guidelines:

For Living Relatives:

  • Obtain permission before including personal information.
  • Avoid full birth dates. Year only is acceptable.
  • Do not include specific current addresses.
  • Respect requests to exclude certain information.
  • Consider using first names only or abbreviations for minor children.
  • Avoid sharing sensitive medical or financial information.

For Family Stories:

  • Ask permission before sharing stories involving living people.
  • Consider the impact of sharing difficult or painful family stories.
  • Respect family members who prefer certain events not be documented.
  • Balance honesty with sensitivity in addressing family challenges.

For Deceased Relatives:

  • Maintain respectful tone when discussing deceased family members.
  • Verify information when possible before including potentially damaging stories.
  • Consider the impact on living descendants.

Ethical Principle: Your family history should honor your family. It should maintain academic integrity and respect for privacy.

Your personal history project involves information that is both private and deeply personal. Before using any AI tool in connection with this project, consider an important data privacy issue: do not paste personal details about living family members into an AI tool. This includes full names combined with birth dates or locations, home addresses, health information, immigration status, or any sensitive family story involving people who are still living. AI tools are external systems, and the information you enter may be stored, used to improve the system, or accessible to others depending on the platform’s policies.

Beyond protecting your family members’ privacy, there is a second reason to work carefully here. Your personal history is meant to be yours, written in your voice, grounded in your memory, and shaped by your reflection. AI cannot know your grandmother’s face or understand why your family’s migration story matters to your identity today. Use AI only for the structural and technical parts of this assignment: to check that your paragraphs flow from one to the next, to confirm that your APA citation for a genealogy database is formatted correctly, or to ask whether your transitions between generations are clear. The stories themselves must come from you.

Organizing Genealogical Data Systematically

Effective organization ensures you can locate and use family information efficiently.

Organization Methods:

Standard Genealogical Format: For each person, record:

  • Full name. Include maiden names for women
  • Birth date and location
  • Marriage date and location if applicable
  • Death date and location if deceased
  • Parents’ full names
  • Children’s names
  • Significant life events or stories

Generational Organization:

  • You are Generation 1
  • Parents are Generation 2
  • Grandparents are Generation 3
  • Great-grandparents are Generation 4

Family Branch Organization:

  • Paternal line includes father’s family
  • Maternal line includes mother’s family
  • Track each branch separately to avoid confusion

Transforming Research into Narrative Form

Your assignment requires narrative prose. It does not allow simply listing names and dates. You must transform your research into engaging writing.

Example: From List to Narrative

List Format (Incorrect): Father: John Michael Smith Birth Date: January 15, 1970 Birth Place: Chicago, Illinois Mother: Sarah Jane Smith

Narrative Format (Correct): My father, John Michael Smith, was born on January 15, 1970, in Chicago, Illinois. He is the son of Robert James Smith and Mary Ellen Thompson Smith. They raised him in the South Side neighborhood. His father worked as a teacher. His mother managed the household while raising four children.

Technique: Convert factual data into complete sentences. These sentences should tell a story about people’s lives. Your personal history should also be presented in full sentences and paragraphs with headings for each section. Do not present information simply in list form.

Using Section Headings Effectively

Your personal history project requires clear section headings. These headings organize your narrative. Remember that this history should be presented in full sentences and paragraphs with headings for each section.

Balancing Factual Information with Storytelling

Effective family history combines necessary facts with engaging narrative elements that bring your ancestors to life.

Your writing should include the required facts as the foundation of your family history. Be sure to include names, dates, and places for each person you write about. State genealogical relationships clearly, so readers understand how people connect with each other. Use chronological progression where appropriate to show how events unfolded. These facts provide the essential structure for your family history.

However, facts alone do not create writing that people want to read. You need to add storytelling elements that transform data into meaningful narrative. Consider including reasons for migrations or moves that explain why your ancestors went where they went. Describe occupations and how they shaped family life and values. Share personal characteristics or defining traits that made each person unique. Explain family traditions or cultural practices that connected generations. Describe challenges your ancestors overcame or achievements they accomplished. Most importantly, connect these experiences between past and present to show how they still influence your family today.

These storytelling elements transform raw data into engaging, meaningful writing that honors your ancestors.

Example of Balanced Writing

“My grandmother, Elena Martinez Garcia, was born in 1952 in rural Oaxaca, Mexico. She grew up in a small farming community. Her family grew corn and beans using traditional agricultural methods. These methods had been passed down through generations. When she was nineteen, she married my grandfather. She moved to Mexico City. This change required significant adaptation. She moved from rural village life to urban complexity. This courage to embrace change influenced how she raised her children. She always encouraged them to pursue education and new opportunities. She encouraged this even when it meant leaving home.”

Connecting Past to Present

Your assignment requires showing how your ancestors shaped your life today. Making these connections helps readers understand why your family history matters.

You can create powerful connections by exploring how values and beliefs passed through generations. For example, you might write something like this: “My grandparents emphasized education even though they had limited schooling themselves. This emphasis shaped my parents’ commitment to ensuring all their children had opportunities to learn and grow.” This approach shows how ancestral values continue to influence your family today.

Cultural practices offer another meaningful way to connect past and present. Consider this example: “The tradition of gathering the extended family for Sunday meals began with my great-grandmother when my family first settled in our community. This tradition continues in our family today and has taught me the importance of maintaining family bonds across generations.” Such connections demonstrate how living traditions link you to your ancestors.

Personal characteristics provide yet another bridge between past and present. You might write: “My grandfather had a reputation for honesty in all his dealings. This established a family expectation for integrity that influences my own approach to ethical decisions today.” This shows how inherited values shape current behavior and choices.

Work ethic and sacrifice also create meaningful connections across time. For instance: “I watched my father work long hours to support our family just as his father had done before him. This taught me the value of persistence and the meaning of sacrifice for those you love.” Such observations illustrate how lessons learned across generations continue to guide you.

Lesson 4.6: Using Sources Ethically and Honestly

In this section, you will learn about APA citation style and explore how different cultures approach citation practices. You will understand your ethical responsibilities as a researcher and discover how to navigate varying academic expectations.

Having explored how to use different types of sources effectively, we now examine the ethical responsibilities that accompany research. Ethical source use connects directly to the revision and editing phase (Step 6) of our research process, where you ensure all sources are properly attributed.

The majority of classes you will take in the future at BYU-Idaho will require you to follow APA style . APA is the documentation and formatting style followed by the American Psychological Association. While other helpful documentations exist, we will focus only on the basic elements of APA in WRIT 101. Please use the BYU-Idaho APA Style Guide as you work on your research projects.

APA Citation at a Glance — Family History Examples

Family history research draws on many different source types — genealogy websites, census records, interviews with relatives, and published histories. Before you open the BYU-Idaho APA Style Guide, use these examples as a quick visual anchor. Every source you use requires both an in-text citation and a matching entry in your reference list.

① IN-TEXT CITATION — BASIC FORMAT ANY SOURCE

Place the author’s last name and year in parentheses at the end of the sentence, before the period.

Format:  (Author Last Name, Year)

Example using a genealogy database:

Birth records confirm that my great-grandfather emigrated from Palermo, Sicily, in 1902 (FamilySearch, 2023).

Example using a published family or local history:

Families in that region frequently relocated during the 1930s Dust Bowl migration (Gregory, 1989).

💡 If the source name appears naturally in your sentence, place only the year in parentheses: FamilySearch (2023) lists my great-grandfather’s arrival date as...

② REFERENCE LIST ENTRIES — THREE COMMON FAMILY HISTORY SOURCE TYPES

Genealogy Website:

FamilySearch. (2023). 1920 United States federal census [Database]. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. https://www.familysearch.org

Published Book (Local or Family History):

Gregory, J. N. (1989). American exodus: The dust bowl migration and Okie culture in California. Oxford University Press.

Personal Interview:

Smith, M. (2024, March 12). Personal interview conducted by [Your Name].

💡 Notice that book and database titles are italicized. Only the first word of a title is capitalized in APA (plus proper nouns). Personal interviews are cited in-text only and do not always appear in the reference list — check your instructor’s preference.

③ PARAPHRASE WITH IN-TEXT CITATION INTERVIEW

When you paraphrase a relative’s words — restating what they said in your own words and sentence structure — you still must include a citation. The idea or memory belongs to them.

What your grandmother said in the interview:

“We left Mexico when I was just a girl. My father said there was nothing left for us there after the drought took the crops two years running.”

Paraphrase with citation:

My grandmother recalled that her family’s decision to leave Mexico was driven by back-to-back crop failures caused by severe drought, leaving her father with no viable path forward in their home region (Garcia, 2024).

💡  Common misconception:  Changing the words does not remove the need to cite. Even a family member’s recollection is their own account — give them credit for it.

Submitting fabricated citations in an academic paper is a form of academic dishonesty, even when there was no intention to deceive. Every citation in your reference list must correspond to a source you have located and read. To verify a citation generated by AI, search for it by title in a library database such as EBSCOhost or Google Scholar, or check the DOI link directly. If the source cannot be found, do not use it.

AI can be a useful tool for understanding how APA formatting works in general — asking it to explain the difference between a journal article citation and a book chapter citation, for example, is a legitimate use. Generating citations for specific sources is not.

Key Terms

APA Style:
The documentation and formatting writing style followed by the American Psychological Association

Citation:
Words or lines taken from a book or a speech and noted in another writing

Dependence Trap:
A prompt or AI use pattern that asks AI to think or decide for the student, replacing learning the task is designed to develop

Draft:
A rough written version of something that is not yet in its final form

Ethics:
Beliefs and principles about what is right and wrong

Growth Accelerator:
A prompt or AI use pattern that asks AI to help the student think more clearly about their own work, strengthening understanding and skill without replacing the student’s effort

Hallucination:
In the context of AI, the production of false or invented information — such as a fabricated citation or a source that does not exist — stated with confidence as though it were accurate

Interview:
Formal meeting at which somebody is asked questions

Paraphrase:
A statement that expresses something that somebody has written or said using different words, especially to make it easier to understand

Primary Sources:
Direct, firsthand sources of information or data like surveys, in-person interviews, and historical documents

Quote:
A group of words taken from a text or speech and repeated by someone other than the original author or speaker

Reference:
How you acknowledge the source of the information you have used (referred to) in your work

Research Question:
A broad, open-ended question that will guide your research research: process of gathering information

Research:
Process of gathering information

Revise:
Process in writing to change something to correct or improve it

Secondary Sources:
Sources that discuss, interpret, analyze, consolidate, or otherwise rework information from primary sources like biographies, literary reviews, or magazine articles

Source:
Place, person, or thing where you get information

Summary:
Statement of the main points

Topic:
A subject or theme

Working Thesis:
A possible answer to your writing’s main research question
