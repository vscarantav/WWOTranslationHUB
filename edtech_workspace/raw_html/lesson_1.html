Academic Honesty, Ethics and Integrity

Welcome to WRIT 101

WRIT 101 is different from most courses you have taken. You will not be answering questions about someone else’s ideas or demonstrating knowledge of a fixed body of content. You will be writing from your own life, your memories, your family history, your experiences, and your voice. That makes integrity in this course more personal, and more important.

This module explains what academic integrity means in a writing course and why it matters. It also covers how to use artificial intelligence responsibly, how to work honestly with sources, and how to collaborate ethically. By the end, you will be asked to reflect on these concepts and take a short quiz. As you read, take note of the principles and examples that feel most relevant to your situation; you will return to them throughout the seven weeks of the course.

You Are a Creator

Elder Dieter F. Uchtdorf (2008) taught that “creation brings deep satisfaction and fulfillment. We develop ourselves and others when we take unorganized matter into our hands and mold it into something of beauty.”

That is exactly what this course asks you to do. In WRIT 101, you will take the raw matter of your own life (your memories, your family history, your experiences, your voice) and shape it into writing that communicates, persuades, and endures. No one else can do that work. The stories in this course are yours. Your thinking is yours alone. The growth is yours.

The Spiritual Foundation

When you choose honesty in all aspects of your life, including every behavior in WRIT 101, you strengthen your relationship with God. When you compromise in any area, you weaken that relationship.

Elder David A. Bednar (2002) taught: “If you leave this university with knowledge and skills and a degree but lacking integrity and honesty, then you have failed.” That standard applies to traditional academic honesty, to how you use sources, to how you collaborate, and to how you use technologies that can do your thinking for you. Technologies change; The Lord’s standards do not.

President Dallin H. Oaks (2003) taught that “the growth required by the gospel plan only occurs in a culture of individual effort and responsibility. It cannot occur in a culture of dependency. Whatever causes us to be dependent on someone else for decisions or resources we could provide for ourselves weakens us spiritually.” Your foundation must be the unchanging gospel of Jesus Christ, not the variable ethics of human society.

What You Might Lose When You Compromise Integrity:
  • Help with learning through the Spirit’s influence
  • Guidance for life decisions through personal revelation
  • The quiet moments where the Spirit can whisper, replaced by algorithmic responses that lack the power to truly transform
  • Confidence in your abilities and peace of mind from living honestly

The Book of Mormon teaches that “by small and simple things are great things brought to pass” (Alma 37:6). Your daily choices about how you use sources, acknowledge assistance, and interact with AI are building the character that determines your future.

Understanding Academic Integrity

Because this course asks you to write about your own life, integrity is not just about following rules. It is about ensuring that what you submit genuinely reflects who you are and what you have learned. The sections below explain each integrity element, what violations look like, and how they connect to the specific assignments you will complete. Students come to this course from many countries and academic traditions, so where those traditions create particular tension with Western academic expectations, that is noted directly.

Proper Source Use and Citation

In WRIT 101, you will use three methods for incorporating sources.

Summarizing condenses a source to its main idea in your own words. Use it when you need the gist without all of the details. Always cite the source you use.

Paraphrasing restates a specific passage in your own words and your own sentence structure. Replacing individual words with synonyms, the “thesaurus method,” is still plagiarism. True paraphrasing demonstrates that you understood the original content well enough to express it completely differently. Always cite your source.

A useful approach: write your own paraphrase first, then ask AI: “Does my version accurately reflect the original meaning, or have I changed anything important?” This requires that you do the intellectual work and use AI to check your thinking rather than replace it.

Quoting Directly reproduces an author’s exact words inside quotation marks with a citation. Use it sparingly when the author’s precise language is vivid or significant. Overuse suggests you are avoiding the harder work of understanding the material yourself.

Fabricated Citations: AI can produce APA-formatted references that look complete and accurate but do not correspond to any real publication. This is called a hallucination. Submitting a fabricated citation is academic dishonesty even without intent to deceive. Every source in your reference list must be a real source you have located and read.

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is presenting someone else’s words, ideas, or creative work as your own, whether intentional or not.

Direct Plagiarism: Copying another person’s exact words without quotation marks or citation.

Example: Copying a paragraph from a website into your Personal History Project without quotation marks or a source acknowledgment.

Mosaic Plagiarism (Patchwriting): Rearranging or slightly rewording someone else’s sentences while keeping their structure. This is plagiarism even when the source is acknowledged. This pattern is especially common among multilingual writers building English fluency, not out of dishonesty, but out of uncertainty about how to express ideas in an unfamiliar language. If you are translating your content, there are approved translation prompts in each assignment that will help you in this process.

Example: Replacing several words in a published sentence with synonyms while keeping the original structure, then submitting it without quotation marks.

Insufficient Citation: Using another person’s idea or argument in your own words without crediting the source. The idea belongs to its originator regardless of how you express it. In many cultural traditions, widely shared community knowledge does not require a formal source, but in Western academic writing, it does.

Ada Okonkwo from Lagos felt this tension when her instructor said that citing “my mother” and “what everyone in Lagos knows about business” was not sufficient, even though her mother had built a thriving business and trained many apprentices. Ada learned to honor both traditions: she found scholarly sources that documented the practices she already knew from experience and wove her personal knowledge alongside them. If you are ever uncertain whether something in your own background requires a citation here, ask your instructor before you submit your assignment.

Example: Paraphrasing a scholar’s argument about storytelling but leaving out the in-text citation, incorrectly assuming that changing the wording means you no longer need to credit the source.

Self-Plagiarism: Submitting work previously submitted without instructor permission.

Example: Submitting a personal narrative you wrote prior to this course for your Week 5 Personal Narrative without letting your instructor know it was previously written work.

AI-Generated Plagiarism: Submitting AI-generated text as your own writing.

Before submitting, ask:

  • Are these my words and ideas, or did they originate with someone else?
  • If they originated elsewhere, have I provided both an in-text citation and a reference list?
  • Would I be comfortable explaining every sentence to my instructor?

Cheating

Cheating means using unauthorized assistance or methods to complete assignments or assessments.

Writ 101 Examples

  • Outside Writing Help: Asking a family member, friend, or tutor to write or substantially revise a draft on your behalf. In many cultures, including many African, Pacific Island, and Latin American traditions, family involvement in education is a natural expression of community care. In WRIT 101, that same care is welcome in the form of encouragement and feedback. The line is this: feedback is appropriate; writing or rewriting your work is not.
  • Contract Cheating: Paying or trading favors for someone else to complete an assignment.
  • Assessment Assistance: Receiving unauthorized help during any individually assessed task.

Fabrication and Falsification

Fabrication means creating false information. Falsification means misrepresenting real information.

Writ 101 Examples

  • Invented Narrative Details: Inserting events, dialogue, or sensory details into a personal narrative that did not actually happen. A personal narrative requires honesty about what you experienced.
  • False Interview Content: Reporting that a family member said something in your Week 4 Family History Research Interview that they did not actually say, or fabricating an interview entirely.
  • AI-Hallucinated Facts: Submitting factual claims, statistics, or citations generated by AI without verifying them. You are responsible for every fact in your submission.

Content Sharing and Seeking

Sharing completed work with others and obtaining completed work from others are violations of academic honesty for both the person sharing and the person receiving are equally responsible.

WRIT 101 Examples

  • Sharing a completed draft in a WhatsApp or Telegram group with other WRIT 101 students to use, even if you intend it only as a model, not as work to be copied
  • Posting screenshots or files of assignments in Facebook groups or other spaces where classmates could access them
  • Obtaining completed assignments from someone who took WRIT 101 in a prior term and submitting them with minor changes
  • Uploading completed work to course-sharing websites such as Course Hero or Chegg, even after the course ends

When in doubt, ask: Did I make the decisions in this draft, or did someone else?

Voice Integrity

Voice integrity is unique to writing courses and deserves its own section.

Your narrative voice is how you sound on the page: the personality and tone that emerge through your writing choices. When you ask AI to “make it better,” it often removes the irregular rhythms and personal patterns that make a voice distinctive and replaces them with smooth, generic prose. The result may look cleaner, but it is no longer yours.

Submitting a narrative that AI has polished into a generic voice is a form of misrepresentation, even when the events described are real. Your instructor assesses your voice as evidence that the writing came from you.

Ada Okonkwo learned this when she asked AI to “improve” her Week 5 narrative and then struggled to recognize her own story in what came back. She revised it with her own voice — the rhythms of her English, the way she described the market in Lagos — and that version was the one her instructor praised.

The approved prompts in each assignment ask AI to identify problems without rewriting anything. If AI returns a rewritten sentence, do not accept it.

AI Ethics and Academic Integrity

Process Tasks vs. Product Tasks

Before using AI on any task, ask: Is this a process task or a product task?

A process task is one where learning and skill development is the purpose. The document you submit is not the goal. Building the ability to produce it is. Every major writing assignment in WRIT 101 is a process task.

A product task focuses on producing a clean, usable result. AI assistance may be appropriate as long as you review the output carefully.

When AI writes your draft or makes rhetorical decisions for you, you may receive a completed document, but you miss the skill the assignment was designed to develop.

The Explainability Test

After every AI interaction, apply this test before moving on:

Can I explain what I submitted to someone else? Could I do it again on my own tomorrow?

If the answer is no, AI completed the task and did not help you learn. This is the primary integrity standard of this course. Your instructor may ask you to explain your work at any time, and you may be asked to share transcripts of your AI conversations. Integrity means being prepared to explain and defend your process honestly.

Feedback Prompts vs. Edit Requests

Asking AI to identify a problem is fundamentally different from asking AI to fix it.

Feedback prompt (keeps the work with you):

“Please identify grammar and spelling errors in this paragraph without changing my ideas.”
"Which sentences could be clearer? Point them out without rewriting.”

Edit request (removes the work from you):

“Rewrite this paragraph to be clearer.”
“Improve my essay.”

When using feedback prompts and AI returns a rewritten sentence, do not just accept the AI suggestions. You should make all of the decisions regarding your work.

The Four Principles

1. Develop Competence

Use AI in ways that build your writing abilities, not replace the effort of developing them. You must be able to perform your work independently. The Parable of the Talents in Matthew 25 teaches this principle clearly: the master rewards those who invested and multiplied what they were given through faithful effort and condemns the servant who buried his talent rather than developing it.

Example: When uncertain how to integrate a quotation, ask AI to explain the technique, then apply it yourself with your own source.
2. Maintain Transparency

President Dallin H. Oaks (2003) taught that “We must change all elements of our behavior that are in conflict with gospel commandments, covenants, and culture.” When you submit work, you claim it reflects your understanding and effort. If AI did the thinking without disclosure, that claim is false.

Every assignment requires one statement at the top:

  • “I did not use AI assistance on this assignment.”
  • “[AI tool name] was used to [specific function, e.g., identify grammar errors]. All ideas, content, and rhetorical choices are my own.”

Be prepared to share AI conversation transcripts.

3. Preserve Your Agency

You make the decisions. AI informs judgment but doesn’t replace it. Elder Bednar (2024) warned that AI’s ease “can create a potentially beguiling, addictive, and suffocating influence on the exercise of our moral agency” and that “We might naively be seduced into surrendering our precious moral agency to a technology that can only think telestial.”

Before any prompt, ask: Is this asking AI to think with me or for me? If for you, rewrite it.

4. Follow Course Policies

Read the AI usage policy in every assignment before beginning. When in doubt, choose the approach requiring more of your own effort. Time pressure doesn’t change policy.

Data Privacy: Protecting Those Who Trust You

AI tools are not private. Never enter full names, contact information, birth dates, immigration status, health information, or sensitive personal stories into AI tools—including family members’ stories from your Personal History Project or Family History Research Interview.

This is an ethical obligation to those who trust you with their stories. If you need AI help with citations involving real people, use initials or “family member” in your prompt.

Appropriate vs. Inappropriate AI Use

Appropriate prompts :

  • “What is the difference between summarizing and paraphrasing?”
  • “What does showing vs. telling mean? Give me an example.”
  • “Can you explain APA in-text citation format for a paraphrase?”
  • “Create five practice sentences for integrating quotations.”

Inappropriate:

  • Having AI write, draft, or substantially compose any assignment
  • Asking AI which details to include in your story, how to adjust your tone, or what your thesis should be
  • Asking AI to paraphrase a source for you, then submitting that paraphrase as your own
  • Asking AI to “improve” or “rewrite” your draft and accepting its changes
  • Submitting AI-generated citations without verifying the sources exist
  • Using AI to generate content for reading journals or spiritual reflections, including the W07 Invitation Reflection

That last point deserves emphasis. Using AI to generate a spiritual reflection robs you of the genuine encounter with God that the assignment is designed to create. An algorithmic response may sound adequate. It cannot transform you.

Dependance Trap Growth Accelerator
Ask AI to write your draft Write your draft first, then ask AI to identify weaknesses
Ask AI which story details to include Make your own decisions, then ask AI to check grammar
Ask AI to paraphrase a source Write your own paraphrase, then ask AI if it reflects the original accurately
Ask AI to "improve" your narrative voice Ask AI where your voice shifts to a generic tone, then revise it back yourself
Ask AI to write your revision reflection Complete your revision plan, document your decisions, then reflect on what you actually chose

Elder Bednar (2024) uses AI this way himself: after finishing a talk, he asks AI to analyze its tone and uses that feedback to make his own improvements. The talk remains entirely his. That is the model for this course.

A Note for Multilingual Students

If English is not your first language, you bring strengths to this course that monolingual students do not have. If AI could help you express an idea more clearly in English while preserving your original meaning, that is the one area where the boundary is more flexible. Your original ideas and experiences are still entirely yours. Always ask your instructor before using AI for translation assistance, and use this approved prompt:

“Help me express this idea more clearly in English while keeping my original meaning and personal voice: [paste specific sentence]”

How Integrity Affects Your Career

Employers want people they can trust with communications, information, and professional relationships.

What Employers Want:

  • Writers who accurately represent their work and sources
  • Professionals who use technology responsibly and ethically
  • Team members who produce original thinking under pressure — the kind AI cannot replicate
  • Employees whose stated skills reflect genuine, demonstrable competence

The habits you build here follow you directly into your résumé. AI-generated résumé content built from generic job titles will not distinguish you from other applicants, and it may be factually inaccurate about your own experience.

Consequences of Compromised Integrity:

  • Academic consequences including assignment failure, or course failure
  • Professional damage when employers or colleagues discover dishonest practices
  • Loss of the authentic writing skills this course is designed to build, skills that only come from genuine effort

Your Opportunity

President Russell M. Nelson (2019) taught that “individual worthiness requires a total conversion of mind and heart to be more like the Lord.” Your commitment to honesty, proper attribution, ethical collaboration, and responsible technology use is part of this conversion process.

As you maintain integrity and engage honestly with your writing, you will develop an authentic voice, one that is yours and no one else’s. You will feel more confident because your abilities are genuine and earned. Living according to your values brings greater peace of mind. Your commitment to honest academic work builds stronger relationships with God and with others.

You are not just completing assignments. You are becoming someone: a writer who finds meaning in experience, a communicator who reaches different audiences, a professional who thinks clearly under pressure, and a disciple who acts with integrity in the small decisions as well as the large ones. AI can be part of that journey. It cannot take the journey for you. You are the creator. AI is the tool. Keep it that way.

Take it into your hands. Write your story. No one else can.
