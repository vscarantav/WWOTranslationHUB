Chapter 1 Writing Project

Overview

  • Task : You will choose a significant personal event and tell it twice — once to a close relationship (friend, family member, or spouse) and once to a professional relationship (coworker, supervisor, or stranger). Then you will analyze how and why you adapted your communication for each audience.
  • Purpose: To practice adapting your writing for different audiences and reflect on how those choices affect communication.
  • Length : 550–800 words total Story 1: 200–300 words Story 2: 200–300 words Reflection: 150–200 words

Instructions

Step 1: Choose and Analyze

Select your significant event.

  • Choose one audience from Group A: close friend, family member, or spouse.
  • Choose one audience from Group B: coworker, supervisor, or stranger. Consider what each audience cares about, what they already know about you, and what tone would be appropriate for each relationship

Step 2: Plan Your Adaptations

Before writing, decide how you will adjust tone (formal vs. casual), language (vocabulary and sentence structure), and appeals (logical, emotional, or credibility-based) for each reader. Consider what details to include, exclude, or present differently.

This step is the intellectual core of the assignment. The decisions you make here are the rhetorical thinking skills this course is designed to develop. You are deciding on tone, vocabulary, types of appeal, and detail selection. Complete this planning step in writing, in your own words, before you consult any resource. AI is not appropriate at this stage. If you ask AI which details to include or how to adjust your tone for each audience, you are replacing the analysis this assignment is designed to build. Your reflection in Step 4 will ask you to explain these choices. If AI made them, you will not be able to explain them in your own words.

Step 3: Write Both Versions

Use this exact format in a single document:

Student Name : [Your Full Name] Event: [Brief descriptive title]

Version 1: [Audience from Group A] Target Audience : [Specify audience] [Your 200–300 word narrative here]

Version 2: [Audience from Group B] Target Audience : [Specify audience] [Your 200–300 word narrative here]

Step 4: Reflect and Analyze

Complete your reflection (150–200 words total) by addressing:

  • Tone Adaptation: How did formality and attitude shift?
  • Language Adjustments: What vocabulary and sentence changes did you make?
  • Appeal Strategies: Did you emphasize logic, emotion, or credibility differently?
  • Detail Selection: What information did you include or exclude for each reader?

Step 5: Requirements Checklist

Before you submit, verify:

  • Two complete story versions (200–300 words each) with clearly different audiences
  • Analytical reflection (150–200 words) explaining your adaptation choices
  • Proper format with required headings (Story 1, Story 2, Reflection)
  • Evidence of conscious tone, language, and appeal adjustments between versions
  • File named “w01_one_story-FirstName_LastName” in .doc, .docx, or PDF format

Step 6: Submit the Assignment

  • Submit your assignment to W01 Writing Project in Canvas, which is due by the end of the week.

Success Criteria

Excellent work will:

  • Demonstrate clear, appropriate differences in tone and language between versions
  • Show thoughtful selection of relevant details for each specific audience
  • Include insightful analysis using Chapter 1 rhetorical concepts
  • Follow formatting requirements precisely and meet word count expectations

English Writing: Mastering the Basics

Run-on Sentences

Just as short, incomplete sentences can create problems, lengthy sentences can create problems too. Sentences with two or more independent clauses that have been incorrectly combined are known as run-on sentences. A run-on sentence may be either a fused sentence or a comma splice.

Fused sentence: A family of foxes lived under our shed young foxes played all over the yard.
Comma splice: We looked outside, the kids were hopping on the trampoline.

Fragments

As you examine your past writing assignments more closely, you may notice that some of your sentences are not complete. A sentence that lacks a subject or a verb gets called a fragment. A fragment may include a description or may express part of an idea. However, a fragment does not express a complete thought.

Fragment: Children helping in the kitchen.
Complete sentence: Children helping in the kitchen often make a mess.

Don’t worry if identifying these problems feels challenging at first. With practice, you’ll develop an intuitive sense for complete, well-constructed sentences.

AI Usage Policy

This assignment is a process task. The rhetorical awareness it develops comes only from making your own decisions. You must practice reading an audience, adjusting your tone, and selecting details. These skills do not transfer when AI performs them for you. AI cannot know your real relationships, your cultural context, or the specific audience dynamics that shaped how you communicate. Asking AI to write either version of your story, or to generate your reflection, replaces this process entirely. Your content, audience analysis, and rhetorical choices must be your own.

After both versions are complete, you may use AI for grammar and spelling identification only. Do not use AI rewrite features, including Grammarly’s suggestion mode, to rephrase your sentences. Accepting AI-generated phrasing changes your voice. It also removes the evidence of your own rhetorical choices, which your instructor will assess in both the stories and the reflection.

PROHIBITED: Having AI write content, ideas, or analysis for you

  • Why prohibited: AI cannot provide authentic personal experiences, genuine audience relationships, or real insights about your own communication effectiveness and rhetorical choices

ASK FIRST: Translation assistance or major editing questions

  • Why restricted: Translation support should preserve your original voice, cultural perspective, and personal meaning in your stories.

APPROVED: Grammar and spelling identification after completing your draft

  • Why allowed: Technical writing support does not compromise your authentic storytelling and helps all students communicate their personal experiences clearly
AI Use Declaration & Attribution

At the top of your submission, include one of the following statements:

  • “I did not use AI assistance on this assignment.”
  • “[AI tool name] helped with [specific function] after I completed my draft. All ideas and content are my own work.”

Please note that you may be asked to describe how you used AI and to share transcripts from your AI conversations. These conversations with instructors are meant to help you understand the appropriate way to use AI to improve your learning.

Approved AI Prompts

Each prompt below is written as a feedback request, not an edit request. This distinction is important. A feedback request asks AI to identify a problem. You decide how to solve it. An edit request asks AI to solve the problem for you. If you use one of these prompts and AI returns a rewritten sentence, do not accept that rewrite. Ask only for identification. Students who write in a second or third language may use the translation support prompt. It is available to help you express your intended meaning in English. Your ideas, experiences, and analysis must remain your own before and after any translation assistance.

  • Grammar Check: “Please identify grammar and spelling errors in this paragraph without changing my ideas or personal details: [paste your text]”
  • Clarity Review: “Which sentences in this paragraph could be clearer? Please point them out without rewriting: [paste your text]”

  • Translation Support: “Help me express this idea more clearly in English while keeping my original meaning and personal voice: [paste specific sentence]”
Why This Assignment Matters

This assignment develops audience adaptation skills you will use throughout your academic and professional career. In workplace settings, you will regularly shift your communication approach for example, presenting financial results to executives versus explaining the same data to your team.
