Making Choices

2 Nephi 2:27 teaches that we are “… free to choose liberty and eternal life.” This freedom of choice represents the gift of agency given to us by our Heavenly Father.

You make choices constantly, and every choice has a consequence. While most of your choices will not have eternal consequences, it is important to make good and wise choices. Even if you are choosing which products to buy, which candidate will get your vote, how you will take care of your health, or what to do with your money, there are potential consequences that will affect you, even if it's indirectly.

Quantitative reasoning strategies can help you make good decisions. However, there is also the risk of becoming misdirected when using reasoning to make decisions.

Logical Reasoning is the process of analyzing and evaluating arguments in order to draw valid conclusions.

Logical reasoning is a key to making good decisions in all aspects of our lives. Last week, you were introduced to the Quantitative Reasoning Process. The purpose of the model is to help you make informed decisions that are based on sound reasoning with quantitative evidence.

Logical reasoning is frequently used to try to influence our decisions. When logical reasoning is used to support a specific conclusion, it is called a logical argument . Sometimes, logical arguments are used to persuade someone to make a choice with a particular outcome, such as an advertiser trying to persuade you to buy their product. Logical arguments present reasons for accepting a conclusion.

Propositions and Conditional Statements

Logical arguments combine one or more propositions and conditional statements.

Propositions

A proposition is a statement that is either true or false.

The following statements are all propositions:

  • “The sun is the center of our solar system.”

  • “Mammals have hair.”

  • “Snakes are mammals.”

  • “Caleb got an A on the exam.”

Notice that each of these statements is either true or false. “The sun is the center of our solar system” is an example of a true proposition, while “snakes are mammals” is an example of a false proposition. Both are propositions because they are statements that are either true or false.

Only statements can be propositions. For example, “Is it raining?” is not a proposition because it is a question instead of a statement. “Hey, you! Come here!” is not a proposition because it is a command.

Conditional Statements

Conditional statements are a particular type of proposition.

A conditional statement is a proposition with an if-then structure.

The following statements are all conditional statements:

  • “If the soccer/football team wins this game, then they will go to the World Cup finals.”

  • “If you take out student loans, then you will have to repay them after you graduate.”

  • “If ye love me, keep my commandments.”

Conditional statements are made up of two parts: the “if” part and the “then” part.

  • The “if” part of a conditional statement is called an assumption.

  • The “then” part of a conditional statement is called a conclusion.

1. Identify Variables & Assumptions

The second step of the Quantitative Reasoning Process is to “identify variables and assumptions.” Understanding that a conditional statement is made up of an assumption and a conclusion helps you apply the Quantitative Reasoning Process correctly. In the example from the previous lesson, Lucas and Amanda make the assumption that their expenses will stay fairly constant until Lucas finishes school. Their conclusions and decisions are based on this assumption. Lucas and Amanda’s assumption can be written as a conditional statement:

“If Lucas and Amanda’s expenses stay fairly constant until Lucas finishes school, then their decision about student loans will be appropriate.”

When making decisions, it is important to know what assumptions you are making. In the case of Lucas and Amanda, if their assumption proves not to be true and their health insurance costs rise suddenly, their expenses will also go up. This affects the decision they make.

Conditional statements are found in computer codes and financial equations. You can also find them in the scriptures, advertisements, and political messages. When the statement involves a message, assumptions are not always stated directly. Some messages assume that everyone believes the same thing.

For example, there was a time when people believed the world was flat. That assumption led to logical reasoning such as, "If the world is flat, then it must have an edge. If there is an edge, then we could fall off the edge of the world. If we try to sail across the ocean, we will fall off the edge of the world." We now know that proposition to be false.

Truth Values of Conditional Statements

Every proposition has a truth value , also called a logical value, meaning it is either true or false. Since conditional statements are one type of proposition, this means that conditional statements can only be either true or false.

Consider the following conditional statement:

“If Anthony takes this pill, then his headache will go away.”

Is this conditional statement true or false?

Without having more information, there isn’t a clear answer. The only way you can know if the statement is true or false is to have Anthony take the pill and see whether or not his headache goes away. If Anthony doesn’t take the pill, then you don't know if his headache would have gone away or not. The correct logic in this case is to conclude that the conditional statement is true. Since there is no evidence that the conditional statement is false, you assume the statement is true.

This is always the case. In order to determine the truth value of a conditional statement, you need to know the truth value of the assumption and the truth value of the conclusion.

In this example, there are four different possibilities:

  • Anthony takes the pill and his headache goes away.

  • Anthony takes the pill and his headache does not go away.

  • Anthony does not not take the pill and his headache goes away.

  • Anthony does not take the pill and his headache does not go away.

Only one of these possibilities makes the conditional statement false. If Anthony takes the pill and his headache does not go away, then the conditional statement is false.

The conditional statement can only be true if Anthony takes the pill and his headache goes away.

If Anthony does not take the pill, we have no way of knowing if his headache goes away. In this case, we assume the conditional statement is true.

This example demonstrates three important principles about the truth value of conditional statements:

  • A conditional statement is false when the assumption is true and the conclusion is false.

  • A conditional statement is true when the assumption is true and the conclusion is true.

  • A conditional statement is assumed to be true when the assumption is false (The conclusion could be either true or false). Because the assumption is false, there is no way to tell whether the conditional statement is true, so we trust that the assumption made in the conditional statement is true.

Conditional statements are called conditional because their truth value depends on whether or not the assumption is true. The assumption tells you the conditions that have to be true for the conclusion to be guaranteed to be true.

In a true conditional statement, if the assumption is true then the conclusion is guaranteed to always be true.

The Quantitative Reasoning Process leads to a conclusion based on a conditional statement. When we make a decision using the Quantitative Reasoning Process, it is only guaranteed to be a good decision if the assumptions we used are true. If our assumptions are false, then our conclusion may or may not be accurate.

Let’s look at a few other examples of conditional statements.

Example 1

Under what conditions would the following conditional statement be false?

“If the soccer/football team wins this match, then they will go to the World Cup final.”

Solution

A conditional statement is false if the assumption is true and the conclusion is false. In this example, that would mean that the soccer/football team wins this match (so the assumption is true) but they don’t get to go to the World Cup final (the conclusion is false).

Example 2

Using the same conditional statement from Example 1, let’s say that the soccer team loses the match, but they still end up getting to go to the World Cup final because their opponent is disqualified. Is the conditional statement true or false?

Solution

The conditional statement as a whole is assumed to be true. In this case, the assumption of the conditional statement is false (they didn’t win the match), so the conclusion may or may not be true (they might get to go to the World Cup final and they might not). Either way, the conditional statement is assumed to be true because the assumption is false. We don’t know what would have happened if they had won the match, so we place confidence in the statement and assume the conditional statement is true.

Example 3

Scriptural Conditional Statement

"Then said Jesus to those Jews which believed on him, If ye continue in my word, then are ye my disciples indeed" ( John 8:31–32 ).

The assumption in this scripture is “if ye continue in my word” and the conclusion is “then are ye my disciples indeed.” Because we know this scripture is true, this tells us if we continue to read, study, and follow the words of the Savior, then we are guaranteed to be His disciples.

Example 4

Scriptural Conditional Statement

"If ye love me, keep my commandments" ( John 4:5 ).

The assumption in this scripture is “if ye love me” and the conclusion is “keep my commandments.” Because we know this scripture is true, we know if we love the Savior, we will keep his commandments.

Strength of Arguments

Logical reasoning is an essential part of the Quantitative Reasoning Process. Understand the assumptions you are making and realize that your conclusions are only guaranteed to be accurate if your assumptions are true. Additionally, use your understanding of this process as a tool to evaluate the persuasive logical arguments you are presented with in the news, social media, and the internet.

A logical fallacy is an argument that is based on an error in reasoning.

There are many types of logical fallicies, but these four are some of the most common:

  • Improper generalization

  • Appeal to emotion

  • Personal attacks

  • Alternative explanations

Improper Generalization

The improper generalization fallacy refers to an argument that concludes that because one individual or group experiences a certain result, then everyone should experience the similar results.

This advertisement is a great example of an argument that includes the improper generalization fallacy.

The advertisement implies that because this program has worked for others, it will work for you too. The ad is not just congratulating the individual on their weight loss, it is inviting us to join them in weight loss through this program.

Some advertisers are clearly aware that their claim is a false generalization. They still make the claim, but to avoid liability, a disclaimer is added that “your results may vary.” The weight loss program may have worked for the individuals referred to in the ad, but that doesn’t mean it will work for everyone. The advertiser is trying to get you to spend your money on their weight control program.

Appeal to Emotion

An appeal to emotion is an argument that claims something is true because of an emotional reason unrelated to the argument.

Here is an example of an emotional appeal. Can anyone resist the dog’s face and pleading look?

This ad includes a picture of a dog and a tagline that says, “Adopt today! A pet is waiting for you.” The image and the words combine to make an emotional appeal encouraging pet adoption. The advertisers are trying to influence your actions by appealing to your emotions.

Personal Attacks

A personal attack fallacy is an argument based on attacking the person making the argument, rather than critiquing the argument on its own merits.

Here is an example of a political ad that uses a personal attack.

This ad is from a State Senator election in Montana, US, in 2014. The ad claims, “Tonya Shellnutt opposes holding rapists and abusers accountable for their crimes.” This is clearly an exaggeration that portrays Ms. Shellnutt in a poor light. It is unlikely that Ms. Shellnutt wants rapists and abusers to have no consequences for their actions, and equally unlikely that she would like Montana women and families to be in danger. The ad attacks Ms. Shellnutt personally because of her position on a particular piece of legislation, instead of addressing the actual law to which it refers.

Alternative Explanations

The alternative explanation fallacy refers to an argument where a conclusion is made without adequately considering other explanations that might explain what is happening.

Did you know that more drownings occur in months where ice cream consumption is high? This must mean that ice cream consumption causes drowning, right?

Rather than making that conclusion, we should consider there may be an alternative explanation.

In this example, you might consider the weather. People tend to eat ice cream more often in warmer weather. People go swimming more often when the weather is warmer. The increase in drowning deaths is probably due to an increase in the number of people swimming during warmer weather and not with eating ice cream.

Conclusion

This lesson highlights the importance of understanding logical arguments when making decisions. In the Quantitative Reasoning Process, we make assumptions. Remember that the validity of the decisions you make are conditional. If the assumptions are true and the conclusion is logically tied to the assumptions, then the conclusions are true.

Be aware of logical fallacies when examining logical arguments. Logical fallacies such as improper generalizations, appeals to emotion, personal attacks, and alternative explanations can lead to invalid conclusions.
