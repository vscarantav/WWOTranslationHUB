Opening Case Study: Bringing Nigerian and American Communication Styles

Ada Okonkwo, a BYU-Idaho Pathway student from Lagos, struggled with her Week 3 assignment on professional email communication. She had written hundreds of work emails successfully in Nigeria. Yet her Pathway instructor marked her practice email as “too indirect” and “overly formal.”

“In Lagos, professional communication requires proper greetings and relationship-building,” Ada explains. “You cannot just ask for what you need immediately. That would be considered rude and disrespectful.”

Her original email assignment to request an assignment extension began like this: “Dear Esteemed Professor, I greet you warmly and trust this message meets you in good health and high spirits. I write to you today with utmost respect and humility regarding a matter of some importance concerning the forthcoming assignment deadline. I pray you will find it in your kind heart to consider my situation, which I outline below with sincere apologies for any inconvenience this may cause your busy schedule...”

The email continued for three more paragraphs before reaching the actual request.

After studying American professional email conventions, Ada learned that U.S. business culture values brevity and directness. She revised her email: “Dear Professor Martinez, I am writing to request a two-day extension for the Week 3 Writing Project, currently due Saturday, October 12. I experienced a family emergency this week that prevented me from completing the assignment on time. I have completed the research and outline, and I am requesting to submit the final draft by Monday, October 14. Thank you for considering my request. Best regards, Adaeze Okonkwo.”

“At first, this felt shockingly rude,” Ada reflects. “In Nigerian culture, you would never be this direct with someone in authority. But I learned that American academic culture interprets this directness as respect for the reader’s time. The challenge is knowing which style to use in different contexts. When I email Nigerian colleagues, I use the relationship-first approach. When I write to American professors, I use the direct style.”

Ada’s experience shows how rhetorical strategies vary across cultures. Consider a related challenge Ada will face in her studies: if she asks an AI tool to write a professional email for her, the tool will most likely produce the direct, low-context American style by default, generating output that may feel correct in one cultural setting and incomplete or impolite in another. Knowing which rhetorical choices to make, and why those choices serve a specific audience, is a skill that AI cannot develop for you. Understanding these differences allows effective communication in diverse contexts.

Lesson 3.1: Audience, Purpose, and Channel

As you write, consider the following questions:

  • Who are your readers?
  • What are you trying to communicate?
  • How will they receive your message?

Knowing the answers to these questions helps you choose which writing tools will best help you communicate your message.

Audience

Everything you write should be done with your reader in mind. You would not write a letter to a future employer the same way you post a message on social media to friends. It is important to craft your message to achieve the desired response from your specific readers.

Research what you know about your audience . Consider aspects like their age, gender, nationality, or education level. Find out what your reader already knows about your topic. Then build your writing from there.

Purpose

After you know your audience, define your purpose in writing your message. Do you want to persuade or motivate others to take action? Do you want to sell something? Would you simply like to inform your audience? Keep that purpose at the focus of your writing. When you do not know your purpose, your writing may not achieve any goal at all. The audience will wonder why they are reading your message.

For example, your purpose in writing about the importance of clean water may be to change the reader’s mind and encourage them to take action. You start by researching the people in your community and recognize that many families with children should demand more from local leaders to protect their water sources. Knowing your audience and your purpose helps you understand how to motivate them toward change and which writing strategies will help you accomplish your purpose.

Channel

Once you know your audience and your purpose, you can identify which writing strategies you should use. Will the audience respond best to emotions or logic? How can you craft your message to effectively accomplish your goals? Think about the reaction you want the readers to have when they read your writing. Do you want them to change their behavior? Would you prefer them to have an emotional reaction? Is the goal simply to educate?

Make a list of the main points and ideas you want to share. Think specifically of your audience. Then, consider the best channel for this writing. Choosing the right channel influences how the reader reacts to your message. An email will be structured differently than a business report. A business report will be structured differently than a post on social media. Consider how formally you want to write to your audience. Should your writing be friendly or more formal? Knowing this helps you decide which strategies you should use.

Responsible AI Use: Audience, Purpose, and Channel

Identifying your audience, your purpose, and your channel is a process task. The thinking you do in this step is the foundation for every writing decision that follows. If you skip this thinking and ask AI to write a message for you, the result may look polished, but it will almost certainly miss audience-specific details that only you know: the relationship you have with the reader, the cultural context shaping the exchange, and the specific purpose behind your request.

A growth accelerator prompt in this context asks AI to test your thinking after you have already done it. For example, after you have identified your audience and purpose yourself, you might write: “I am writing a professional email to request a schedule change from my supervisor. My audience is a manager who values advance notice and specific details. My purpose is to propose a reduced schedule with a clear end date. Does this audience and purpose analysis seem complete, or am I missing something?” This kind of prompt uses AI to challenge your reasoning without replacing it.

The dependence trap, by contrast, asks AI to do the analysis for you before you have attempted it: “Tell me who my audience is and what my purpose should be for this email.” This prompt removes the core thinking of the lesson from the task. Your ability to identify audience, purpose, and channel independently is one of the foundational skills of this course and of professional communication in any country.

Professional Email Communication

Email serves as the primary communication channel for professionals worldwide. Each day, approximately 361 billion emails are sent and received. Understanding professional email conventions helps you communicate effectively in academic and workplace settings.

Email Structure and Format

Professional emails follow a clear structure that helps readers quickly understand your message and respond appropriately. Every professional email should include these essential components:

Subject Line: The subject line tells your reader what the email is about before they open the message. Use the format [Action or Topic] + [Specific Detail] to create clear subject lines. Examples:

  • “Question About Assignment 3 Due Date”
  • "Request for Extension: Week 5 Writing Project”
  • “Grade Inquiry: Midterm Exam”

Avoid vague subject lines like “Question,” “Help,” or “Hi” that do not provide useful information.

Greeting: Begin your email with a professional greeting that addresses your recipient appropriately. For academic contexts, you can use “Dear Professor [Last Name]” for the most formal approach, “Hello Doctor [Last Name]” for a professional and friendly tone, or “Good morning” or “Good afternoon Professor [Last Name]” for a polite and time-aware greeting.

Body - Three-Part Structure:
  • Context: Briefly explain who you are and why you are writing (one to two sentences)
  • Request: State your specific request clearly and directly (one to two sentences)
  • Appreciation: Thank the reader for their time and consideration (one sentence)

Closing: End your email with a professional closing phrase. You can use “Sincerely” for formal situations, “Best regards” for professional standard communication, or “Thank you” when requesting assistance.

Signature: Include your full name, student ID number, and course section.

Common Email Mistakes to Avoid

When writing professional emails, be aware of common formatting errors that can undermine your message. These include writing in all lowercase letters, using excessive punctuation like multiple exclamation points or question marks, forgetting to include a subject line, and not including your full name and course information.

Tone problems also damage professional communication. Avoid being too casual with greetings like “Hey” or “What’s up,” being overly apologetic with multiple apologies throughout your message, using demanding language such as “I need you to...,” and including too much personal detail.

Self-Assessment: Email Writing Checklist

Before sending a professional email, verify:

  • Subject line is specific and informative
  • Greeting is professional and includes recipient’s name
  • First sentence identifies who you are and your course
  • Request is stated clearly and specifically
  • Necessary context is provided without excessive detail
  • Professional closing phrase is included
  • Signature includes full name, student ID, and course section
  • Email has been proofread for spelling and grammar errors
  • Tone is respectful and professional throughout

A Note on AI and Professional Email

Drafting a professional email is a product task. Its purpose is to produce a clear, appropriately formatted communication, and AI can help you produce a starting draft. You must review that draft carefully before sending it, because AI commonly makes errors specific to email writing. It may produce a tone that is too formal or too casual for your actual relationship with the reader. It may include incorrect details such as the wrong date, course number, or assignment name, stated with confidence as if they were accurate. It may generate a culturally generic message that does not reflect the relational norms of your specific context.

Before you send any AI-assisted email, apply the Facts, Tone, Privacy, and Purpose checklist from your course AI module. Verify every specific detail such as dates, names, and course sections. Confirm that the tone matches your relationship with the reader. Check that you have not shared unnecessary personal or private information. Confirm that the email accomplishes your actual purpose. If you cannot confirm all four items, revise until you can.

Communication Strategies Across Cultures

How people communicate varies greatly across cultures . This is particularly true when it comes to persuasion . Consider these examples:

Japanese Communication Style: In Japan, indirect communication is often valued over directness. Persuasive messages frequently begin with background context, gradually build toward the main point, and avoid direct requests. The concept of “reading the air” means understanding unspoken expectations and context.

Kenyan Griot and Storytelling Traditions: Many Kenyan communities value storytelling as a persuasive technique, presenting arguments through narratives that illustrate principles rather than through direct statements or data alone. This tradition shares roots with the West African Griot tradition, in which storytellers in countries like Mali and Senegal use praise poetry and historical narratives to influence community decisions. Griots establish credibility through genealogical knowledge and use repetitive structures with call-and-response formats to engage audiences directly in the persuasive process.

Brazilian Business Communication: Brazilian business communication often establishes personal relationships before focusing on business matters. Meetings might begin with personal conversation, and persuasive appeals frequently blend emotional connections with logical arguments.

German Rhetorical Approach: German communication tends to value directness, thoroughness, and expertise. Persuasive messages often begin with credentials and detailed information, and presentations typically include comprehensive background before conclusions are presented.

Maori Whaikorero (Formal Speaking): In New Zealand’s Maori culture, formal speeches follow specific protocols including honoring ancestors, acknowledging the land, and connecting personal stories to community values. Speakers use poetic language and genealogical references to establish authority and emotional connection with listeners.

Indigenous North American Council Speaking: Many Indigenous nations use circular speaking patterns where each person contributes to collective decision-making. The emphasis is on consensus-building rather than winning arguments. Speakers often begin by acknowledging their relationship to the community and the land, establishing ethos through connection rather than individual expertise.

Ethiopian Debate Traditions: Ethiopian academic and religious traditions include sophisticated debate formats where scholars engage in structured arguments using specific logical progressions. These debates emphasize intellectual rigor while maintaining respect for opposing viewpoints. Understanding these diverse approaches enhances your ability to communicate globally and recognize that effective persuasion looks different depending on cultural context.

Lesson 3.2: Rhetorical Appeals and Persuasion Strategies

Persuasive situations often involve rhetoric, which is the craft of using language to motivate audiences to action. In Week 1 you were introduced to ethos, pathos, and logos. This week we examine all four rhetorical appeals in depth, including kairos, and learn how to apply them strategically.

Rhetorical Appeals

Four essential strategies help you persuade others:

  • Ethos is establishing credibility and trust. You show expertise or good character to make your audience believe you. Example: Mentioning your experience or credentials.
  • Pathos is appealing to emotions. You connect with feelings your audience already has. Example: Describing how a problem affects real people.
  • Logos is using logic and evidence. You provide facts, reasons, and examples that lead to a sensible conclusion. Example: Sharing statistics or research findings.
  • Kairos is recognizing the right timing. You understand when your audience is most receptive to your message. Example: Proposing a solution when a problem is urgent.

Responsible AI Use: Identifying and Applying Rhetorical Appeals

Learning to identify and apply ethos, pathos, logos, and kairos is a process skill. The practice of reading a message, naming which appeals are at work, and deciding which appeal fits your specific audience is what builds rhetorical judgment over time. This judgment is what instructors, employers, and colleagues will evaluate in your professional communication.

A growth accelerator prompt in this context asks AI to evaluate a choice you have already made. For example: “I used a pathos appeal in this message by describing how water shortages affect families with young children. Is this an effective choice for an audience of local business owners, or would a logos appeal be stronger here? Explain your reasoning.” This prompt asks AI to test your strategic thinking without making the decision for you.

A dependence trap, by contrast, removes the decision entirely: “Tell me which rhetorical appeal to use in my message.” The first prompt asks AI to respond to your thinking. The second prompt replaces your thinking with AI’s output. The rhetorical choices you practice making in this lesson are the same choices you will need to make independently in your academic and professional writing.

Practice Example: Analyzing Rhetorical Appeals in a Speech

Let’s analyze how Martin Luther King Jr. used multiple rhetorical appeals in his famous “I Have a Dream” speech:

Ethos (Credibility):

  • He established himself as a minister by using Biblical references and religious language
  • He referenced American founding documents to align himself with respected traditions
  • He positioned himself as a representative of the civil rights movement

Pathos (Emotion):

  • He used evocative imagery: “We will not be satisfied until justice rolls down like waters and righteousness like a mighty stream"
  • He appealed to universal hopes for children’s futures: “little black boys and black girls will be able to join hands with little white boys and white girls as sisters and brothers”
  • He created emotional contrast between current struggles and future hope

Logos (Logic):

  • He presented logical argument that America had failed to fulfill its promises to all citizens
  • He structured his speech with clear cause-and-effect reasoning
  • He used historical evidence and facts to support his arguments

Kairos (Timing):

  • He delivered the speech during the March on Washington when national attention was focused on civil rights
  • He referenced the Emancipation Proclamation’s 100th anniversary
  • He spoke at the Lincoln Memorial, connecting to Lincoln’s legacy of freedom

This analysis shows how effective persuasion often combines multiple rhetorical appeals to create a powerful, compelling message.

Literary Language

You can also use more subtle strategies with your choice of language and organization:

  • Figurative language: similes and metaphors. Comparing one aspect of things that in other ways are completely different is an essential part of rhetorical language. Simile example: “The treasure chest of nature’s wonders shone like a bright golden object.” Metaphor example: “The pizza was a disk of saucy sunlight.”
  • Numerical data: statistics and figures. When accurate, numerical data can strengthen an argument.
  • Parallel structure: repetition of the same pattern of words to show that ideas are equally significant. Parallel structure calls attention to these ideas. It achieves balance. It makes the statements more memorable. Example from the inaugural address of United States President John F. Kennedy: “Ask not what your country can do for you - ask what you can do for your country.”
  • Personification: giving an inanimate or nonhuman object human characteristics to make it seem alive and relatable. Examples: “The virus packed its bags and spread across the ocean” or “The internet erupted in outrage.”
  • Repetition: repeating a single word or group of words to build emphasis. Example: “The first cause is poverty; the second cause is poor health; the third cause is discrimination. These causes have been studied, but to what effect?”
  • Rhetorical question: a question that is not expected to be answered. It is a question that creates a dramatic effect. Examples: “Has it occurred to you to ask why the economy is so unstable? A first point to consider is . . .”; “Do you think poverty will go away by itself?”
  • U nderstatement: presenting something as less important than it is as a way of distancing from the truth. Understatement is often used sarcastically or ironically. Example: “It may not have occurred to politicians that poverty leads to a host of health-related issues.”

Applying Rhetorical Appeals in Brief Messages

The rhetorical appeals you have learned (ethos, pathos, logos, kairos) work effectively in brief messages as well as in longer texts. Understanding how to use these appeals in short-form communication helps you persuade others in everyday situations like text messages, social media posts, and quick emails.

Adapting Appeals for Short Messages

Brief messages require you to make strategic choices about which appeals to emphasize, since you have limited space and must select the most effective approach for your specific audience and purpose.

Character and word limits significantly affect your strategy choices. Text messages and social media posts have strict length limits, so you must convey your persuasive message in one to three sentences. This requires careful word choice and focus on your strongest appeal.

The Four Appeals in Brief Format

Let’s examine how each rhetorical appeal works in a short message. We will use the same scenario: inviting a friend to attend a worship service with you this weekend.

Ethos (Credibility) Example:
“Hey! I’ve been attending this church for six months now, and it’s really helped me with stress and focus. Would you want to check it out with me this Sunday?”

Why this works: The message establishes credibility through personal experience. Your friend knows you have firsthand knowledge of the church. The mention of specific benefits (stress relief, focus) shows you understand what the experience offers. Your extended attendance (six months) demonstrates commitment and suggests the experience is valuable.

Pathos (Emotion) Example:
“I’ve found such a welcoming community at my church, and I’d love to share that experience with you. It would mean a lot to me if you’d come this Sunday!”

Why this works: The message appeals to emotional connection and belonging. Words like “welcoming community” and “share that experience” create positive feelings. The phrase “it would mean a lot to me” directly appeals to your friend’s care for your feelings and your friendship.

Logos (Logic) Example:
“My church has a great young adult group that meets at 10 a.m. on Sundays. They serve brunch afterward, and there’s always interesting discussion. Want to come this week?”

Why this works: The message provides logical reasons to attend. The timing (10 a.m.) is reasonable. The mention of brunch adds practical value. The reference to “interesting discussion” suggests intellectual engagement. These facts give your friend concrete reasons to say yes.

Kairos (Timing) Example:
“There’s a special guest speaker at my church this Sunday who has an amazing personal story. This would be a perfect week to visit if you’re interested!”

Why this works: The message emphasizes the unique timing of this specific invitation. “Special guest speaker” and “amazing personal story” suggest this Sunday offers something different from a typical service. “Perfect week to visit” creates a sense of opportunity that might not come again soon.

Combining Appeals in Limited Space

The most effective brief messages often combine multiple appeals. When you have very limited space, consider which appeals work best together for your specific situation.

Effective Combination Example

“I’ve been going to this church for months (ethos) and found such great community there (pathos). They have a young adult group that meets Sunday at 10 (logos). There’s a special speaker this week (kairos). Want to come?”

This message combines all four appeals in three sentences. Each appeal adds a different dimension to the invitation.

Respectful Religious Communication

When inviting others to religious or worship services, maintain respect for different beliefs and backgrounds through these important principles.

Consider your friend’s beliefs and comfort level first. Some people have strong religious beliefs different from yours, while others may have negative past experiences with religious communities. Respect these factors in how you extend your invitation.

Focus on sharing personal experience rather than arguing beliefs. Talk about what the experience has meant to you (ethos and pathos) rather than trying to convince your friend that certain beliefs are correct (logos). Your invitation should share, not pressure.

Make genuine invitations by accepting “no” as a valid response. A true invitation allows the other person to decline without feeling guilty, so avoid repeated invitations if someone has already declined.

Logical Fallacies

Some rhetorical strategies should be avoided because they rely on flawed reasoning. These are also known as logical fallacies . These strategies undermine your logical argument. The argument may sound convincing, but the logic is flawed. The flawed logic may distract from the real issue.

Fallacy Name What It Does Example
Ad Hominem
against the person 		Attacks the character or identity of the person making an argument instead of addressing the argument itself.
“Only ignorant and cruel people don’t support clean water initiatives.”
Bandwagon
appeal to popularity 		Argues that because many people do or believe something, you should too, using popularity as a substitute for evidence.
“Everyone is voting for clean water. You don’t want to be left behind, do you?”
Causal Fallacy
false cause 		Claims that because one event followed another, the first event must have caused the second, without proving a real connection.
“Your children won’t grow tall if we don’t clean our water sources soon.”
H yperbole
exaggeration 		Overstates a claim so dramatically that it distorts the truth and undermines the credibility of the argument.
“If we do not value clean water, our lives are not worth living.”
Slippery Slope
chain reaction fallacy 		Assumes that one action will automatically lead to a chain of extreme consequences, without showing how or why each step would occur.
“Drinking unclean water will always lead to an early, painful death.”
False Dilemma
either/ or fallacy 		Presents only two extreme options as if no middle ground or other alternatives exist, forcing an unnecessary choice.
“Either you support this water bill completely, or you don’t care about public health at all.”

Common Misconception: All Emotional Appeals Are Fallacies

Many people incorrectly believe that any appeal to emotion is a logical fallacy. This is not true. Emotional appeals become fallacious only when they are used to bypass logical reasoning entirely. They become fallacious when they manipulate feelings without substantive arguments.

Valid emotional appeal: “Consider how water contamination affects children’s health. Studies show that lead exposure can cause permanent cognitive damage. This damage affects their ability to learn and thrive.”

Fallacious emotional appeal: “If you don’t support this water treatment facility, you clearly don’t care about children’s suffering.”

The first example uses emotion to highlight legitimate concerns backed by evidence. The second example uses emotion manipulatively to shame people without addressing the merits of the argument.

Lesson 3.3: Rhetorical Analysis and Creating Persuasive Messages

Now that you understand how to write using these tools, you also need to know how to recognize these tools used by other writers through a process known as rhetorical analysis.

Rhetorical analysis means breaking down how persuasion works by examining the strategies writers use. Look at the individual parts of writing to understand how language works and creates the effects the writer wants. A rhetorical analysis should examine the conditions of communication that surround the rhetoric, including the author, the audience or readers, the main message and purpose, the channel being used, and where and when the message is being sent.

If you were doing a rhetorical analysis, you might ask these questions as you read:

  • What are the goals of the author of the text?
  • What rhetorical strategies, literary language , or logical fallacies did the author use to persuade?
  • How did the author use timing to help make the point?
  • How will the audience react? Will readers agree with this writing because they are of the same opinion? Are they hostile and ready to reject the arguments? Have they heard or read the ideas before?
  • Are readers likely to see the author as familiar or as a stranger who must win their confidence?

Step-by-Step Guide to Rhetorical Analysis

  1. Identify the rhetorical situation
    • Who is the author?
    • Who is the intended audience?
    • What is the context (historical, social, cultural)?
    • What is the purpose of the communication?
  2. Examine the appeals used:
    • Ethos: How does the author establish credibility?
    • Pathos: How does the author appeal to emotions?
    • Logos: How does the author use logic and reasoning?
    • Kairos: How does timing affect the message?
  3. Analyze the structure and style
    • How is the message organized?
    • What literary techniques are employed?
    • What tone does the author use?
    • How do word choices affect the message?
  4. Evaluate effectiveness
    • How well do the rhetorical strategies work for the intended audience?
    • Are there any logical fallacies or weaknesses?
    • Does the message achieve its purpose?
  5. Consider cultural context
    • How might cultural factors influence how the message is received?
    • Would these strategies work across different cultural contexts?

Responsible AI Use: Rhetorical Analysis

AI can play a useful supporting role after you have completed your own analysis. A growth accelerator prompt sounds like this: “I analyzed the Smokey Bear campaign and identified personification, emotional appeals targeting families, and hyperbole in the slogan ‘Only you can prevent forest fires.’ Are there additional rhetorical strategies I may have missed, or do you see gaps in my analysis?” This prompt shares your completed thinking with AI and asks for a critique, keeping the analytical work with you while using AI to strengthen it.

A dependence trap sounds like this: “Analyze the rhetorical strategies in the Smokey Bear campaign.” This prompt produces an answer you can read but cannot own, because the thinking behind it was never yours. When you write a full rhetorical analysis, draft your analysis before consulting AI, and review any AI feedback against your assignment instructions rather than treating AI’s response as the final word.

The Workings of Rhetorical Analysis

The aim of rhetorical analysis is not to find agreement with or praise for the writer. You are looking for the effectiveness of the writing. You do this by identifying the writer’s tools and what they accomplish.

As you formulate your rhetorical analysis, be aware of the approaches and strategies that writers use to persuade an audience. Your goal will be to identify these strategies in your analysis. You will explain their use. You will evaluate their effectiveness.

Establishing Credibility

Writers include their credentials or experience with the subject. This ensures that readers will take them seriously as someone who knows what they are talking about. To reinforce their authority, writers cite reliable sources as support for their points.

Sharing Personal Experience

Sharing a personal experience related to the subject enhances credibility. The personal experience may also appeal to readers’ emotions.

Targeting Emotional Concerns

The author can rally readers to take a particular position by specifically addressing those incidents or outcomes that readers may fear or desire. Emotional concerns also include appeals to the five senses. They include broader sentiments such as love, loyalty, anger, justice, or patriotism.

Supporting Claims with Convincing Evidence

Ways of supporting claims include quoting, summarizing, or paraphrasing expert opinions. Writers also relate anecdotes and examples. They cite appropriate statistics and facts.

Acknowledging the Opposition

Writers may make a point of explaining other groups’ positions carefully and respectfully. When they do this, readers from those groups, as well as the target audience, are more likely to be responsive to the writer. By acknowledging the opposition, writers show they have considered opposing views. They can then demonstrate that their position is preferable.

Creating Your Own Persuasive Messages

You have learned how to analyze persuasive messages created by others. Now you will learn how to create your own effective persuasive messages. This section provides step-by-step guidance for applying rhetorical strategies to your own writing.

From Analysis to Creation

Analyzing rhetoric helps you understand how persuasion works, while creating rhetoric requires you to make strategic choices about your own communication. You must consider your specific audience, purpose, and context to select the most effective strategies.

The creation process differs from analysis in important ways. Analysis identifies what others have done, while creation requires you to make original choices. Analysis evaluates effectiveness after the fact, while creation must anticipate audience response in advance.

Step-by-Step Process for Creating Persuasive Messages

Follow these steps to create effective persuasive messages:

Step 1: Define Your Rhetorical Situation

Answer these questions before you begin writing:

  • Who is my specific audience? (not just “people” but specific individuals or groups)
  • What do I want them to think, feel, or do after reading my message?
  • What is my relationship to this audience?
  • What channel will I use? (email, text, speech, social media, formal paper)
  • What constraints do I face? (length limits, time limits, format requirements)
Step 2: Analyze Your Audience

Understand your audience’s characteristics:

  • What do they already know about my topic?
  • What are their current attitudes toward my topic?
  • What values and priorities influence their decisions?
  • What objections or concerns might they have?
  • What communication style do they prefer?
Step 3: Choose Your Rhetorical Appeals

Select which appeals to emphasize based on your audience and purpose:

  • Choose Ethos when: Your audience questions your credibility, you need to establish expertise or trustworthiness, your character and values matter for this topic, or you are addressing an audience unfamiliar with you.
  • Choose Pathos when: Your audience needs emotional motivation to act, the issue involves human experiences or values, logical arguments alone seem insufficient, or you want to create a strong connection with your audience.
  • Choose Logos when: Your audience values facts and reasoning, the decision involves practical considerations, you have strong evidence and data to support your position, or your audience is skeptical and needs convincing.
  • Choose Kairos when: Timing creates unique opportunity or urgency, current events relate to your message, your audience is particularly receptive right now, or a deadline or time-sensitive factor exists.
Step 4: Draft Your Message

Write your first version incorporating your chosen appeals:

  • Begin with the appeal you want to emphasize most
  • Include specific details that support your chosen appeals
  • Keep your audience and purpose in focus throughout
  • Use appropriate tone for your relationship and context
  • Follow format conventions for your chosen channel
Step 5: Evaluate Your Own Rhetoric

Review your draft using this checklist:

  • Ethos Evaluation:
    • Have I established my credibility appropriately?
    • Have I demonstrated knowledge of the subject?
    • Have I shown respect for my audience?
    • Does my tone match the relationship and context?
  • Pathos Evaluation:
    • Have I connected to emotions appropriately?
    • Are my emotional appeals genuine, not manipulative?
    • Have I considered my audience’s values and concerns?
    • Does my message inspire the desired emotional response?
  • Logos Evaluation:
    • Have I provided sufficient evidence and reasoning?
    • Are my arguments logical and well-organized?
    • Have I avoided logical fallacies?
    • Have I addressed potential objections?
  • Kairos Evaluation:
    • Is this the right time to send this message?
    • Have I created appropriate urgency without pressure?
    • Have I referenced relevant current context when helpful?
    • Does my message suit this particular moment?
  • After you have completed your own evaluation using the four checklists above, you may ask AI to review your draft for rhetorical effectiveness, give AI your completed evaluation alongside the draft, and ask it to identify gaps in your reasoning rather than rewrite the message for you.
Step 6: Revise for Effectiveness

Make improvements based on your evaluation:

  • Strengthen weak appeals
  • Remove or revise manipulative language
  • Add missing evidence or emotional connection
  • Improve clarity and organization
  • Adjust tone if needed

Practice Example: Creating a Persuasive Message

Let’s follow the process to create a message. Aiyana from Manitoba needs to request a schedule change at the community center where she works:

Step 1 - Define Rhetorical Situation:
  • Audience: My direct supervisor at the community center
  • Purpose: Get approval for reduced weekend hours during exam period
  • Relationship: Professional, respectful, established over six months
  • Channel: Email
Step 2 - Analyze Audience:
  • Supervisor values reliability and advance notice
  • Supervisor has approved flexible arrangements for other student workers
  • Supervisor prefers clear proposals with specific details
  • Supervisor appreciates workers who suggest solutions
Step 3 - Choose Appeals:
  • Logos (primary): Emphasize advance notice and proposed coverage plan
  • Ethos (secondary): Reference my reliable attendance record
  • Light kairos: Mention upcoming exam schedule creates need
Step 4 - Draft Message:
RE: Request for Schedule Adjustment: Exam Period
Dear Ms. Thompson,
I am writing to request a temporary schedule adjustment for December 10-20 during my exam period. I would like to reduce my weekend hours from 16 to 8 per week during this time.
I have arranged for another student worker to cover the additional hours. My exams are scheduled for December 12, 15, and 18. The reduced schedule would allow me to study adequately while maintaining my commitment to the community center.
Over the past six months, I have maintained perfect attendance and received positive feedback from program participants. After exams, I will resume my regular schedule on December 21.
I am happy to discuss this request at your convenience. Thank you for considering my proposal.
Best regards, Aiyana Blackbird
Step 5 - Evaluate:
  • Ethos: ✓ References reliable attendance record appropriately
  • Pathos: ✓ Minimal emotional appeal, focuses on responsibility
  • Logos: ✓ Strong practical arguments about coverage plan, specific dates, commitment to return
  • Kairos: ✓ Mentions exam schedule to explain timing
Step 6 - Revise:
  • Message is effective as drafted. No major revisions needed.

Rhetorical Strategies in Advertising

Let’s look at an example of rhetorical analysis using the Smokey Bear advertisement.

Smokey Bear has been the symbol of wildfire prevention since 1944 in the United States. The image reflects the use of rhetorical strategies. Smokey Bear is a symbol created in 1944 to raise awareness of the danger of forest fires. Images of this gentle, personified bear are often accompanied by the slogan “Remember . . . only you can prevent forest fires” or a variation of the slogan.

The continuing identification of the bear and his appeal over decades is an example of the powerful use of rhetorical devices. These devices speak without seeming to become dated and lose impact. First, a wild and dangerous animal is personified and made credible. The credibility of Smokey as a domesticated father figure with fuzzy, playful cubs climbing on the family mailbox removes any sense of danger. Instead, the image makes Smokey into a believable voice for safety.

The images are emotionally accessible to children as well as adults. They appeal to widely shared emotions. The unspoken implication is that preventing forest fires will allow these young animals and forest plants to live rather than die in a carelessly started and deadly fire. In addition, preventing forest fires will allow human life to continue safely and pleasurably.

Yet the most frequently used slogan, “Only you can prevent forest fires,” is an example of hyperbole. Certainly “you” are not the sole person responsible for starting or preventing fires. Other people and other factors are at work aside from yourself.

When you perform a rhetorical analysis, you see the strategies of personification , emotional appeals, credibility, and even hyperbole. What other strategies do you see as you study this campaign?

Workplace Communication Applications

Modern professionals must adapt rhetorical strategies across multiple platforms and contexts. Here are key applications:

Email Communication:

  • Ethos: Professional signature, clear subject line, proper formatting
  • Logos: Bullet points for key information, data attachments, clear action items
  • Pathos: Appropriate tone (urgent vs. routine), acknowledgment of recipient’s time
  • Kairos: Send timing that respects recipient’s schedule and workload

Social Media for Business:

  • LinkedIn posts: Establish credibility through industry expertise and professional achievements
  • Twitter updates: Use logical arguments with supporting links and data
  • Instagram stories: Appeal to emotions through visual storytelling and behind-the-scenes content
  • Platform adaptation: Formal tone on LinkedIn, conversational on Twitter, visual on Instagram

Presentation Delivery:

  • Opening: Establish credibility and connect with audience interests
  • Body: Use logical progression with supporting evidence and emotional examples
  • Closing: Call to action that leverages timing and urgency
  • Cultural adaptation: Direct style for Western audiences, context-building for Asian audiences

One development worth noting in professional communication: AI tools now generate persuasive content at scale. Marketing emails, social media posts, customer service responses, and even news summaries may be produced by AI systems trained to use the same rhetorical appeals you are studying in this chapter. The rhetorical analysis skills you are developing here serve a dual purpose. They help you craft effective messages for your own audiences. They also help you recognize when a message you receive has been designed to persuade you, whether that message was written by a person, an AI, or some combination of both. The ability to identify ethos, pathos, logos, and kairos in the messages that arrive in your inbox, your social media feed, and your workplace communication channels is a form of critical literacy that becomes more valuable as AI-generated content becomes more common.

Case Study Follow-Up: Applying Chapter Concepts

Looking back at Ada’s experience at the beginning of this chapter, we can now see how the rhetorical concepts you learned apply to her challenge:

Audience Analysis: Ada learned that her Nigerian instructors valued gradual development and context-building, while her American instructors valued direct, clear arguments. Understanding these different audience expectations helped her adapt her writing style appropriately.

Rhetorical Appeals: In her original approach, Ada relied heavily on ethos (establishing respect and credibility through formal language) and pathos (showing consideration for multiple viewpoints). Her revised approach emphasized logos (clear, logical argument structure) while maintaining appropriate ethos through professional tone.

Cultural Communication: Ada’s experience demonstrates that effective communicators can adapt their rhetorical strategies based on cultural context. She learned to use relationship-first approaches with Nigerian colleagues and direct argumentation with American academic audiences.

Purpose and Channel: Ada discovered that the same message (her position on online education) required different rhetorical strategies depending on the academic context and cultural expectations of her audience.

Practical Application: Today, Ada successfully code-switches between communication styles. Her ability to use both Nigerian and American rhetorical approaches makes her a more effective global communicator. She honors both cultural traditions while meeting the expectations of different academic and professional contexts.

Ada’s success story demonstrates that learning new rhetorical strategies does not mean abandoning your cultural communication values. Instead, it means expanding your toolkit to communicate effectively with diverse audiences while maintaining your authentic voice. When Ada writes professional emails today, she drafts them herself first, using the rhetorical judgment she developed through practice, and then asks AI to evaluate whether her tone and structure match the cultural expectations of her specific reader, rather than asking AI to produce the message for her. Her competence in code-switching is hers because she built it.

Key Terms

Audience
The people giving or likely to give attention to something

Channel
A medium for communication or the passage of information

Culture
Beliefs, laws, customs, and habits of a group

Ethos
Using credibility or character to appeal to your audience

Kairos
Timing when conditions are right for the accomplishment of a crucial action

Literary Language
A writer’s choice of language and organization

Logical Fallacies
Reasoning where invalid arguments or irrelevant points are introduced without any evidence to support them

Logos
Appealing to your audience’s logical side

Pathos
Appealing to your audience’s emotions

Personification
Assigning human characteristics to an inanimate object

Persuasion
The act of persuading someone to do something or to believe that something is true

Purpose
The intention, aim, or function of something; the thing that something is supposed to achieve

Rhetoric
The craft of persuading through writing or speaking

Rhetorical Analysis
Breaking down how persuasion works by examining the strategies writers use
