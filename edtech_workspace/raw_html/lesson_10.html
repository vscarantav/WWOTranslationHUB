Chapter 3 Writing Project

Overview

  • Task: Write the same message twice to the same person, each version using a different rhetorical appeal, then analyze which version would work better and why.
  • Purpose: To practice using a variety of rhetorical appeals to effectively make a case.
  • Length : 350–400 words total

What This Assignment is Asking You to Do

You will write two versions of the same message to the same real person you know. Both versions have the same goal: either to invite that person to attend a Sunday church service or another church event or to make a professional request. The difference is that each version uses a different rhetorical appeal to make the case.

After writing both versions, you will analyze each one and then compare them, explaining which you would send and why.

This assignment is asking you to think like a communicator who adjusts strategy based on audience, not just tone. The goal is not to write two polished messages. The goal is to understand why one approach works better than another for this specific person.

Example

The following example shows all five parts of the assignment at the required length. Your person, scenario, and analysis will be different. Use this example to understand the format and the level of thinking each part requires.

Part 1: Scenario and Audience

Scenario : Professional request asking a former supervisor to serve as a job reference

Recipient : My former supervisor Mr. James Okafor, who managed me at a logistics company for three years before I left to continue my education. Our relationship was respectful and productive. He gave me strong performance reviews and once told me he hoped I would return to the industry. I have not been in regular contact with him for about eight months. He is detail-oriented and values professionalism. He is originally from Nigeria and tends to respond well to messages that are clear, direct, and show respect for his time.

Part 2: Appeals Selected

Version A : Ethos (Reminding Mr. Okafor of my professional record and growth establishes that the request is credible and worth his time.)

Version B : Logos (Giving him specific, practical information about what the reference requires makes the request easy to evaluate and easy to say yes to.)

Part 3: The Two Messages

Both messages are written as emails to the same person, Mr. Okafor.

Version A (Ethos)

Dear Mr. Okafor, I hope this message finds you well. My name is Fatima Al-Rashid, and I had the privilege of working on your logistics coordination team from 2020 to 2023. I am currently applying for a supply chain analyst position at Meridian Freight Solutions and am reaching out to ask whether you would be willing to serve as a professional reference.

During my time on your team, I completed the regional inventory audit ahead of schedule and earned the department’s reliability recognition two years in a row. I believe your perspective on my work ethic and attention to detail would strengthen my application significantly. I would be honored to have your support. I am happy to provide any information about the role that would help you prepare.

Respectfully,
Fatima Al-Rashid

Version B (Logos)

Dear Mr. Okafor, I hope you are doing well. My name is Fatima Al-Rashid, and I worked on your logistics coordination team from 2020 to 2023. I am applying for a supply chain analyst role at Meridian Freight Solutions and would like to list you as a professional reference.

The reference process would require a brief phone call or a short written statement, estimated at 15 to 20 minutes of your time. The application deadline is March 14, so I would need to confirm your availability by March 7. I have attached a summary of the role and a copy of my current resume so you have everything you need.

Please let me know if you are comfortable moving forward. I am grateful for your consideration.
Respectfully.
Fatima Al-Rashid

Part 4: Analysis of Each Message

Version A Analysis

Version A uses ethos by leading with Fatima’s professional track record before making the request. Naming specific accomplishments, the regional inventory audit and the reliability recognition, reminds Mr. Okafor of concrete reasons to speak positively on her behalf. This matters because eight months have passed since they last communicated, and a reference request after a gap of contact can feel presumptuous without that context. For Mr. Okafor, who values professionalism and detail, grounding the request in documented performance is more persuasive than a general appeal to the relationship. The final line offering to provide information about the role also signals that Fatima respects his time and has thought ahead.

Version B Analysis

Version B uses logos by giving Mr. Okafor every practical detail he needs to make a decision: the estimated time commitment, the format of the reference, the application deadline, and a clear action date. Nothing is vague. My audience analysis tells me Mr. Okafor is detail-oriented and responds well to clear, direct communication, so removing uncertainty from the request reduces friction. Attaching the role summary and resume means he does not have to ask follow-up questions. For a busy professional receiving a request from someone he has not heard from in months, a well-organized message signals respect for his time more effectively than an emotional appeal would.

Part 5: Reflection

Both versions are appropriate for Mr. Okafor, but I would send Version B. His detail-oriented communication style means that a clearly structured request with specific information is more likely to produce a quick, confident response than one that relies on reminding him of past performance. Version A is strong, but it asks him to recall specific accomplishments from years ago, which puts the work of remembering on him. Version B gives him everything he needs in the message itself.

The eight-month gap in contact also matters here. Reestablishing the relationship through credentials, as Version A does, could feel like justifying the request rather than simply making it. For a professional who already holds me in good regard, the more respectful move is to make his decision as easy as possible.

Instructions

Use the five-part structure above. Choose a real person and a real scenario. Your analysis should reflect your genuine understanding of that person, not a generic description of an audience type.

Step 1: Scenario and Audience (no word count, use the template)

  • Use these exact headings:
    • Scenario: State whether you are writing a church event invitation or a professional request, and describe it in one sentence.
    • Recipient: Describe the real person you are writing to. Include their role or relationship to you, their communication preferences as you understand them, relevant personality traits, and any cultural background that might influence how they receive a message. Write three to five sentences.

Step 2: Appeals Selected (no word count, use the template)

  • Use these exact headings:
    • Version A: Name the appeal you will use and write one sentence explaining why you chose it for this person.
    • Version B: Name the appeal you will use and write one sentence explaining why you chose it for this person. The two appeals must be different. You may choose from ethos, pathos, logos, and kairos.

Step 3: The Two Messages

  • Write both messages to the same person for the same scenario. Label each one clearly.
  • If you are writing text messages, write 25–50 words each.
  • If you are writing emails, write 100–150 words each.
  • Use the same format (both texts or both emails) for both versions.

Step 4: Analysis of Each Message (75–100 words each)

  • Write a separate analysis for each message. For each one, explain specifically how you used the rhetorical appeal, point to at least one sentence or phrase where that appeal appears, and explain whether this approach would work for your specific audience and why.
  • Do not summarize what the message says. Analyze the strategic choices you made.

Step 5: Reflection (100–125 words)

  • Compare both versions. Explain which one you would actually send to this person and why. Your explanation should connect to your audience analysis from Step 1, not just general preferences. Address how cultural factors or the nature of your relationship influenced your conclusion.
