Opening Case Study: The Power of Strategic Reading

Ada Okonkwo, a BYU-Idaho Pathway student from Lagos studying through Pathway, faced unexpected challenges with her course readings. Despite speaking English her entire life and earning her diploma in English, she found herself re-reading paragraphs multiple times without fully understanding them.

“I thought something was wrong with me,” Ada explains. “I speak English at work, I read English newspapers, and I passed my diploma exams in English, but these American textbooks were difficult in a way I didn’t expect. The style is more conversational”

Ada’s confusion stemmed from differences between Nigerian English and American academic English. Phrases like “hit the ground running” or “level playing field” appeared in her business textbook without explanation. Cultural references to American holidays, historical events, and social contexts assumed knowledge she did not have. Sentence structures in academic texts differed from the English she learned in Lagos.

After learning several strategic reading techniques, Ada’s approach changed dramatically. “Now I preview each text before reading. I look up unfamiliar phrases and make notes about American cultural references. I use annotation strategies to mark sections where the English feels different from Nigerian English. When I need specific information, I scan instead of reading every confusing word.”

Her breakthrough came during Week 2 when she realized she could leverage her multilingual skills. “I speak Igbo, English, Nigerian Pidgin, and some Yoruba. I constantly translate between languages and cultural contexts. These same skills help me ‘translate’ between Nigerian English and American academic English.”

The results were remarkable. Ada reduced her reading time while significantly improving her comprehension. Ada also discovered one way AI could support her process without replacing it: when she encountered an unfamiliar American idiom or cultural reference, she asked AI to explain the phrase in context, then added a margin note in her own words, keeping the annotation and the thinking her own. “I wish I had learned earlier that my English being ‘different’ does not mean it is ‘wrong.’ Now I see my multilingual background as an advantage.”

AI and the Reading Process: Growth Accelerator or Dependence Trap?

Reading for education is a process task. The value is not in having a summary of a text. The value is in the thinking you do as you move through it. This distinction matters when you consider how AI tools might help or harm your development as a reader.

A growth accelerator prompt uses AI to deepen your engagement with a text you have already read. For example, after previewing a chapter and identifying unfamiliar terms, you might ask: “I am reading an academic article about public health policy. Can you explain what the phrase ‘social determinants of health’ means in plain language?” That prompt supports your reading without replacing it. A dependence trap prompt skips the process entirely: “Summarize this chapter for me so I understand it.” That prompt hands over your comprehension work to AI, and you lose the very skill the assignment is designed to build.

Before using AI in connection with any reading assignment in this course, ask yourself one question: Have I engaged with this text myself first? If the answer is no, put the AI tool aside and begin reading.

Reading for Education vs. Pleasure

Reading for education is different from reading for pleasure. Academic reading does not consist of only looking at a text once and then putting it away. Instead, successful reading in education requires engaging with text multiple times to gain a deeper understanding of the concepts.

A college degree or certificate means hundreds of hours spent reading. This section will help you use those hours most productively. You will learn strategies for active reading that help to focus your learning process. These strategies will improve your reading skills so you have a better sense of what to do before, during, and after you read.

Good writing begins with good reading. Every time you read, you are exposed to someone else’s ideas and to their way of writing. You see their word choice, vocabulary, knowledge, and use of language. You can become a better reader by reading every day and practicing effective reading skills.

Reading effectively means reading in a way that helps you understand, evaluate, and reflect on a written text. As you might guess, these skills are very important to college students. No matter what field you are going into, you will be doing a lot of reading. The more effectively you read, the easier it will be. It will also take less time, and you will enjoy the experience more.

Types of Reading Purposes

Understanding your purpose for reading determines which strategies you should use:

Academic Reading: Reading to learn, analyze, and apply new concepts in educational settings. This type requires deep comprehension and critical evaluation.

Professional Reading: Reading to make informed decisions, solve problems, or stay current in your career field. This includes reports, emails, industry publications, and technical documents.

Civic Reading: Reading to understand community issues and evaluate political information. You also read to make informed decisions as a citizen. This includes news articles, ballot measures, and public policy documents.

Personal Reading: Reading for spiritual growth, entertainment, or personal development. This includes scriptures, self-help books, and recreational literature.

The Complete Reading Process Loop

Before we examine individual strategies, let’s understand the complete framework for effective academic reading. Research by Graves, Cooke, and LaBerge (1983) shows that strategic reading involves four interconnected steps that form a continuous cycle. Rather than reading a text once and moving on, effective readers loop through these steps repeatedly — previewing , questioning , annotating , and reviewing — with each pass deepening their comprehension and retention.


Four-Step Reading Process Loop:

  • Step 1: Previewing - Examine text features before detailed reading to activate prior knowledge and set reading purposes
  • Step 2: Questioning - Create dialogue with the author by actively thinking about and challenging the text
  • Step 3: Annotating - Engage physically with the text through marking, note-taking, and making connections
  • Step 4: Reviewing - Reflect on and consolidate learning after reading to ensure understanding and retention

This process is recursive, which means you can move back and forth through different steps as you work. You might preview a section, read and annotate it, then preview the next section before continuing. Keep reading and thinking about what you have read until you feel comfortable with the material.

Lesson 2.2: Previewing and Questioning

Step 1: Previewing

Previewing is the first step in the reading process loop . This strategy helps readers recall prior knowledge and set a clear purpose for reading by examining text features before detailed reading begins.

Previewing calls for readers to skim a text before reading, looking for various features and information. This information helps when they return to read in detail later.

Think about the word itself in parts. “Pre” is a prefix that means “before.” The word “viewing” means to see. You are thinking about how to “see” before you read.

Research shows that previewing a text can improve your comprehension by helping you set a purpose for your reading and prepare for what comes next.

How to Preview Different Text Types

Depending on the genre, or the kind of reading, your approach to previewing may vary:

  • Previewing nonfiction: Find out what you know about the subject and what you want to find out. This helps you understand how an author has organized information.
  • Previewing biographies: Identify key information about the person, including the time period they lived in and significant places or events that shaped their life.
  • Previewing fiction: Determine characters, setting, and plot to make predictions about a story’s problems and solutions.

Previewing Digital vs. Print Texts

For Print Texts:
  • Examine the table of contents and index
  • Read chapter headings and subheadings
  • Look at images, charts, and captions
  • Read the introduction and conclusion
  • Check for special features like callout boxes
For Digital Texts:
  • Scan the navigation menu or site structure
  • Use browser search (Ctrl+F) to locate key terms
  • Check for multimedia elements like videos or interactive features
  • Look for hyperlinks that provide additional context
  • Note the publication date and author credentials for credibility assessment

Source Evaluation During Previewing

Modern readers must evaluate the credibility of digital sources. Use these criteria when previewing online content:

  • Authority: Who wrote this content? What are their credentials?
  • Currency: When was this published? Is the information up to date?
  • Purpose: Why was this created? Is it informational, persuasive, or commercial?
  • Accuracy: Can you verify facts through other sources?

Evaluating AI-Generated Source Information

The same criteria you use to evaluate any online source, including authority, currency, purpose, and accuracy, apply directly to AI-generated content. AI tools can produce plausible-sounding summaries of articles, authors, and research findings that do not exist. This type of error is called a hallucination, and it occurs when an AI generates confident-sounding information that is fabricated.

When you use AI to help you locate or evaluate sources, apply your previewing skills to the AI response before you trust it. Check the author’s credentials against an actual institutional or publication website. Verify publication dates by finding the original source. Never cite a source that AI has suggested without first confirming that it exists and that it says what AI claims.

This is not a reason to avoid AI during the research and reading process. Your previewing skills, including examining authority, currency, purpose, and accuracy, are exactly the tools you need to keep AI working for you rather than misleading you.

Step 2: Questioning

Building on the previewing foundation, the second step in the reading process loop is active engagement through questioning . Being an active reader means that you should be actively thinking about the text you read. You should be interacting with it as if it were a conversation.

Just like a conversation includes a back-and-forth dialogue between two speakers, when you read a text, there should be a dialogue between you and the author. Imagine that you were in an argument with a friend. While they speak, you are thinking about what they are saying. You may also be formulating a response in your mind. You could also be thinking of questions to counter the things they are saying.

When you read, you similarly want to think of it as a conversation. Think about what the author is saying. Develop your own thoughts and questions about what you read, which is an excellent way to think critically about a text. Asking questions helps you become an engaged reader.

Types of Questions to Ask

As you read, consider these important questions:

  • What is the author’s main point?
  • What evidence supports this claim?
  • How does this connect to what I already know?
  • What questions does this raise for me?
  • Do I agree or disagree with this perspective? Why?

Write down your answers to help you engage with the text and prepare for class discussions.

Lesson 2.3: Annotating and Reviewing

Step 3: Annotating

The third step in the reading process loop involves the physically engaging with the text. One effective way to do this is through annotation — pausing to reflect, marking important concepts, and adding notes as you read. This active engagement not only improves your comprehension of the material, but also helps you retain what you’ve read, ultimately saving you time by reducing the need to re-read the material.

Beyond Simple Highlighting

The simplest ways to annotate is by marking up the text. You can highlight, underline, bracket, or place symbols in the text or the margins. But simply highlighting is insufficient. Highlighting tells you that you thought something was important the moment you read it. But when you go back later, you will not know why you thought it was important.

As you annotate, you will also want to add keywords, phrases, or questions, so you make connections to the content.

Common Misconception: Highlighting is the Best Way to Annotate

Many students believe that highlighting or underlining key passages is sufficient for effective annotation. While highlighting can be useful for noting important sections, it is often overused. It provides little benefit on its own.

When you return to a text where you’ve only highlighted, you may not remember why you thought those sections were important. Without additional notes, the highlighted sections lose their context and significance.

More Effective Approach: Instead of just highlighting, add brief margin notes explaining why the passage is important. Use a simple annotation system like:

  • “?” for confusing sections
  • “!” for important points
  • “C” for connections to other concepts
  • “D” for definitions
  • “E” for examples

While annotating , look for opportunities to:

  • Add examples from real life, other books, TV, movies, and so forth

  • Comment on the actions or development of characters

Digital Annotation Strategies

Today’s readers navigate multiple platforms, each requiring a different approach to annotation.

Digital Annotation Tools: Many digital tools are available to help you annotate across different platforms and file types.

  • Web browsers with extensions like Hypothesis or Diigo let you highlight and add notes directly onto web pages without altering the original content

  • Note-taking apps that sync across devices (OneNote, Notion, Obsidian)

  • Turn off notifications on devices
  • Use reader mode in browsers to eliminate ads and pop-ups
  • Set specific times for checking hyperlinks rather than following them immediately

Using Personal Knowledge and Pacing

Though college involves a lot of reading subjects or topics that are new to you, your prior knowledge can still help. Try to connect readings to what you already know. For example, you might be reading for your public health class and then remember a relative’s heart attack. What you learned as your family member went through the treatment could help you relate to the reading.

Breaking your reading into shorter sessions can help you stay focused and think more critically. You know your own reading and thinking capacities better than anyone else, so don’t push ahead if you find yourself no longer reading critically. This is also why it is important to start reading assignments early so you leave yourself enough time to step away and come back if needed.

Step 4: Reviewing

The fourth and final step in the reading process loop is about consolidating and reflecting on what you’ve learned. Once you’ve finished reading, ask yourself these questions to help you process the material, and write down your answers. If certain passages were difficult, revisit them with these questions in mind.

  • What did I learn?
  • Who can I ask for help if anything is still unclear?
  • Did I find the author’s style persuasive? Why or why not?
  • Why do I agree or disagree with what I read?
  • How does this connect to other things I am learning about?
  • When have I read anything on this topic before?

This reviewing step ensures that reading becomes learning, not just information consumption. It helps you retain what you’ve read and prepare for future application in assignments, discussions, and exams.

Lesson 2.4: Scanning, Skimming, and Global Reading Perspectives

In addition to the four-step reading process loop, you should also master two specialized strategies: scanning and skimming .

Scanning

Scanning is used to find specific information quickly. Run your eyes over the text looking for the specific piece of information you need. You may run your eyes quickly down the page in a zigzag or a winding S pattern.

Are you looking for a name? You’ll then note capital letters. Are you looking for a date? You’ll look for numbers. Vocabulary words may be boldfaced or italicized. When you scan for information, you read only what is needed.

Tips for Effective Scanning:

  • Scan for titles, headings, and subheadings
  • Scan the first sentence after each heading
  • Scan any supplemental material at the beginning or end of the text
  • Use internet tools like search bars or Ctrl + F

Skimming

Skimming is used to quickly gather the most important information. Run your eyes over the text, noting important information. You can use skimming to quickly get up to speed on the basic content coverage. It is not essential to understand each word when skimming.

Tips for Effective Skimming:

  • Skim the first paragraph or introduction
  • Skim the last paragraph or summary
  • Skim the abstract if one is provided

Reading Across Cultures: Global Perspectives

Reading strategies vary across cultures and educational systems. Here are some approaches from different global regions:

Japanese “Gyosho” Annotation Method: Students make vertical annotations in the margins of texts. They use specific symbols to mark questions, connections, and important concepts. This method emphasizes concise notetaking while preserving the flow of reading.

Brazilian Reading Circles: In Brazilian universities, students often form “círculos de leitura” ( reading circles). Each participant focuses on a specific aspect of the text. They examine main arguments, evidence, or methodology. Then they share their insights with the group.

Nigerian “PQRST” Method: This adaptation of the Western SQ3R method uses the steps of Preview, Question, Read, Summarize, and Test. Nigerian educational systems place particular emphasis on the summarization step.

Korean “Chunking” Strategy: Korean students often break down difficult texts into conceptual “chunks.” They focus on mastering one section completely before moving to the next.

These diverse approaches demonstrate that effective reading strategies can be adapted to different cultural contexts. Consider which elements from various traditions might enhance your personal reading practice. The key principle remains consistent across cultures: active engagement with text produces better comprehension than passive reading.

Cross-Skill Connections: Reading Strategies for Better Writing

The reading strategies you master this week directly support your writing development. When you learn to question and annotate as a reader, you develop critical thinking skills that improve your own writing. When you learn to recognize effective organization and evidence in others’ writing, you can apply these techniques to your own work.

Reading-to-Writing Transfer

From Previewing to Planning: Just as you preview texts to understand their structure, you can outline your own writing to create clear organization for readers.

From Questioning to Argument Development: The critical questions you ask while reading help you identify strong versus weak arguments, improving your ability to construct persuasive writing.

From Annotating to Revision: Your annotation skills transfer directly to editing your own work, helping you identify areas that need clarification, evidence, or improvement.

From Reviewing to Self-Assessment: The reflection skills you use after reading help you evaluate your own writing objectively and identify areas for improvement.

Key Terms

Active reading:
Reading with a determination to understand and engage with the text rather than passively consuming information. Active reading involves questioning, annotating, and making connections between the text and your existing knowledge. This approach increases comprehension and retention compared to passive reading.

Annotating:
Actively engaging in a text by pausing to reflect, markup, and add notes as you read. Annotation goes beyond highlighting to include writing questions, making connections, summarizing key points, and creating dialogue with the author. Effective annotation creates a record of your thinking process that aids review and revision.

Literacy:
The ability to read and write effectively in academic and professional contexts. Modern literacy includes not only basic reading and writing skills but also the ability to critically evaluate information, understand complex texts, communicate clearly across different formats and audiences, and adapt to digital communication platforms.

Previewing:
A reading strategy that helps readers recall prior knowledge and set a clear purpose for reading by examining text features before detailed reading begins. Previewing involves looking at titles, headings, introductions, conclusions, and visual elements to understand the text’s structure and main focus, improving subsequent comprehension.

Questioning:
Actively thinking about and interacting with the text you read, creating a dialogue between you and the author. Effective questioning involves asking about the author’s purpose, evaluating evidence, making predictions, and challenging assumptions presented in the text to deepen critical engagement and comprehension.

Reading Process Loop:
A systematic approach to reading that involves four interconnected strategies: previewing, questioning, annotating, and reviewing. This recursive process allows readers to move back and forth between strategies as needed to achieve deep understanding rather than reading text only once passively.

Scanning:
Running your eyes over text in a systematic pattern to locate specific information such as titles, vocabulary words, dates, or key ideas. Scanning is used when you need particular pieces of information rather than complete understanding of the entire text, often employing zigzag or S-pattern eye movements.

Skimming:
Reading quickly over text to gather the most important information or main ideas without focusing on every detail. Skimming helps readers determine the overall content and structure of a text before deciding whether to read it thoroughly, typically focusing on first sentences, headings, and introductory and concluding paragraphs.

Vocabulary:
The range of language and terminology used by a particular author, group, or academic discipline. Building vocabulary improves both reading comprehension and writing ability, as understanding precise word meanings enhances your ability to process complex ideas and express yourself professionally across different contexts.
