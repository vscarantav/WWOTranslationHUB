Opening Case Study: Learning Directness in Academic Writing

Ada Okonkwo, a BYU-Idaho Pathway student from Lagos, struggled with her first writing assignment. She was a good student in secondary school. Now, taking Pathway courses online while working full-time, she felt confused by her instructor’s feedback.

“In Nigeria, we are taught to build up to our point gradually,” Ada explains. “You establish context, show respect for other viewpoints, and guide your reader to the conclusion. When my instructor said my main argument was ‘buried’ on page two, I did not understand. In my previous education, this approach was considered sophisticated and respectful.”

Ada’s original writing included many qualifying phrases and built slowly toward her main point. After working with her instructor, she learned that American academic writing values directness. She revised her writing to state her main argument clearly and early.

“At first, this felt rude and abrupt,” Ada reflects. “But I learned that directness is not disrespect in academic writing. It helps readers understand your argument immediately. Now I see how my Nigerian communication style and American academic expectations can both be valuable, but I need to know when to use each approach.”

Ada’s experience demonstrates how educational and cultural backgrounds shape writing approaches. Ada’s situation also illustrates a temptation many global students face asking AI to rewrite a draft in American academic style so the argument sounds direct without the student having to learn why directness is valued or when to apply it, which is precisely the skill her instructor was trying to help her build. Understanding these differences helps writers adapt while also honoring their cultural identity. The writing principles you’ll learn this week can help you successfully navigate these challenges.

Lesson 1.1: Writing Clear and Concise Sentences - AI, Growth, and Dependence in Writing

You already write sentences every day. You write texts, emails, messages to family, and notes to yourself. You do this naturally and without much thought. Academic writing asks you to do the same thing more intentionally: to choose your words carefully so your reader understands exactly what you mean, without wasting their time.

This lesson focuses on two foundational sentence skills. The first is writing concisely. The second is writing clearly. These two skills will shape everything else you produce in this course. Before you begin, there is one important idea to understand about how this lesson works and why the practice matters.

Writing and AI: Why This Lesson Requires Your Own Work

In most subjects, AI assistance produces a result that is clearly separate from your own work. In writing, the line is less obvious. When you ask AI to rewrite a sentence, the ideas may have started with you. However, the words, the structure, and the choices that shape how a reader receives your meaning now belong to the AI.

That distinction matters most in a skills-based lesson like this one. Learning to write concisely is not about producing a clean sentence. It is about developing the ability to recognize wordiness in your own writing. This is a skill you will use in every professional document, email, and report you write for the rest of your career. If AI shortens your sentences for you, the task gets done, but the skill never develops

This lesson introduces a distinction you will encounter throughout the course.

A growth accelerator keeps the work with you. It asks AI to observe or identify a problem in writing you have already done, then leaves the fixing to you. Here is an example: "Which words in this sentence could be cut without changing the meaning? Do not rewrite it. Just point them out." You read the feedback, make the decision, and revise. The skill stays with you because you are doing the work.

A dependence trap gives the work away. Here is an example: "Make this sentence more concise." The sentence gets shorter, but you did not do the thinking that builds the skill. The next time you face a wordy sentence on your own, you will be no better prepared than before.

As you work through each section below, you will see specific guidance on where AI can support your learning and where it will replace it. The habit to build now is simple: do the work first, then use AI to respond to what you have done, not to do it for you.

The difference between the two becomes especially important in writing because writing tasks feel collaborative with AI in a way other tasks do not. A growth accelerator in writing sounds like this: “Here is a paragraph I wrote. Does the topic sentence control the whole paragraph, or does the paragraph shift focus partway through? Do not rewrite anything, just tell me what you observe.” A dependence trap sounds like this: “Rewrite this paragraph so it has a clearer topic sentence.” The first prompt keeps the writing work with you. The second gives it away.

Academic writing also develops your voice, the specific way you organize and express your thinking that reflects your experience, your reasoning, and your perspective. Voice is not a stylistic preference. It is evidence that you did the intellectual work. AI-generated writing does not carry your voice. Instructors who know your writing will recognize when a submission does not sound like you. Employers who hire you for communication roles will expect you to produce work under conditions where AI is not available. The skills this course builds are portable in a way that AI-assisted results are not.

The guidance embedded in each lesson this week will show you specifically where AI can support your development as a writer and where it will substitute for it. The principle is consistent throughout: use AI to get feedback on work you have already done, not to produce work you have not yet attempted. That single habit protects your learning in every writing task this course asks you to complete.

Writing Clear and Concise Sentences

You already write sentences naturally every day in texts, emails, and notes. Academic writing simply requires you to be more intentional about your sentence choices. Think of this section as fine-tuning skills you already possess.

Effective writing is clear and concise at every level, from overall structure to individual word choice and punctuation. Every word or phrase should have a purpose.

Write Concise Sentences

As you write, always ask yourself how your writing impacts your readers’ time. Do you write unnecessarily complex sentences? Do you use 50 words when five would convey your message? Some sentences may appear plain and direct. However, sentences do not need additional words. Flowery words and phrases obscure your ideas. When writing, being concise remains key.

For example, instead of writing “Cats have a tendency toward sleeping most of the day,” you could simply write, “Cats usually sleep most of the day.” Consider changing “The 12th day of the month of April” to “April 12th.” These changes seem small. However, they make your writing clearer and stronger.

Try to identify such sentences and substitute simpler ones. This skill will improve with practice. AI can shorten a sentence for you in seconds. The problem is that if AI does the shortening, you do not develop the ability to recognize wordiness in your own writing, which is the skill this lesson is designed to build.

A growth accelerator in this context sounds like this: “Here is a sentence I wrote. Please tell me which words or phrases could be cut without changing the meaning, but do not rewrite the sentence for me: [paste sentence].” That prompt asks AI to identify the problem. You then decide how to fix it.

A dependence trap sounds like this: “Make this sentence more concise: [paste sentence].” That prompt hands the skill to AI. After several exchanges like that, you will produce cleaner sentences only as long as AI is present, which is not the same as learning to write clearly. Remember, you’re not “dumbing down” your writing. You’re making it more powerful by removing distractions.

When you write succinctly, you want to avoid unnecessary words. For example, you do not need to talk about the “positive benefits” of a program in your community. The word “benefits” already implies that it remains positive.

Write Clear Sentences

You might worry that your sentences do not sound professional enough. You might feel like you need to use complex vocabulary instead of using your natural language skills. This remains common, but remember that complex words and complicated sentences are not always better. Clear sentences represent strong sentences.

Global perspective: In international business, simple and clear English often communicates more effectively than complex academic language. A software engineer in Brazil, a marketing manager in Kenya, and a finance director in Thailand all benefit from straightforward communication.

Vary the Length of Your Sentences

Your sentences should vary in length. Avoid having too many long sentences. Long sentences take longer to read and often appear more complex. Reserve short sentences for main points. Use longer sentences for supporting points that clarify a message or explain cause and effect relationships. Some sentences may feel too long. Break these sentences into two sentences. You do not want your reader to have to read a sentence twice to understand it. (All of the techniques are present in this paragraph).

This variety keeps your reader engaged and helps emphasize your most important points. Think of sentence length like music. You need both short, punchy notes and longer, flowing melodies to create an interesting composition.

Practice Example: Sentence Revision Process

Wanjiku’s first draft: “Due to the fact that there are a large number of students who are in the process of learning about technology for the very first time during this particular time period, the computer lab has made the decision to extend its operating hours.”

Problem identified: Wordy phrases, unnecessary words

Wanjiku’s revision process:

  • “Due to the fact that” → “Because”
  • “a large number of students who are” → “many students”
  • “in the process of learning” → “learning”
  • “for the very first time” → “for the first time”
  • “during this particular time period” → (delete - unnecessary)
  • “has made the decision to” → “will”

Wanjiku’s final version: “Because many students are learning about technology for the first time, the computer lab will extend its operating hours.” (18 words vs. 38 words)

Lesson 1.2: Topic Sentences

A topic sentence states the main idea of a paragraph . Just as a thesis statement focuses on an entire essay, a topic sentence controls a single paragraph. Topic sentences usually appear at the beginning of paragraphs, though they can appear elsewhere.

What Makes a Good Topic Sentence?

A strong topic sentence should:

  • State one clear idea that the paragraph will develop
  • Connect to your thesis statement by supporting or explaining part of your main argument
  • Be specific enough to guide the rest of the paragraph
  • Be general enough to need further explanation or support

Examples of topic sentences

Weak topic sentence: “Online education has many aspects.”

Problem: Too vague - what aspects? How will you develop this?

Strong topic sentence: “Online education provides flexibility that helps working adults balance their studies with job responsibilities.”

Why it works: Specific focus (flexibility for working adults) that can be developed with examples and explanations.

Practice Example: Developing Topic Sentences

Let’s follow student James from Canada as he develops topic sentences for an essay about smartphone benefits:

James’s thesis: “Smartphones improve student productivity through organization tools, communication features, and learning applications.”

Topic sentence 1: “Organization tools on smartphones help students manage their academic schedules effectively.”

Topic sentence 2: “Communication features allow students to collaborate easily with classmates and instructors.”

Topic sentence 3: “Educational applications provide students with instant access to learning resources.”

Analysis: Notice how each topic sentence supports a different part of James’s thesis. Each one introduces a specific benefit that he can develop with examples and evidence.

Now that you understand how topic sentences focus on individual paragraphs, let’s take a closer look at how to use them to build effective paragraphs.

Lesson 1.2: Building Effective Paragraphs with Topic Sentences

The paragraph serves as the building block of essay writing. You already use paragraphs naturally when you text or email friends. For instance, when you shift to a new idea, you probably start on a new line. In academic writing, paragraphs serve the same purpose but with more intentional structure.

Paragraphs break information into manageable chunks that are easier for readers to process. This approach works much better than presenting information in one large block of text, which becomes hard to read.

A paragraph is composed of multiple sentences focused on a single, clearly defined topic . There should be one main idea per paragraph. Whenever you move on to a new idea, you start a new paragraph.

After the topic sentence introduces the main idea, the remainder of the sentences in a paragraph should support or explain this topic. These additional sentences might detail your position on the topic. These sentences might also provide examples, statistics, or other evidence to support that position.

Practice Example: Constructing an Effective Paragraph

Let’s follow student Maria Santos from Brazil as she constructs a paragraph about smartphone benefits:

Step 1: Start with a clear topic sentence.

Maria’s topic sentence: “Smartphones help students stay organized and manage their time effectively.”

Step 2: Add supporting evidence.

Maria thinks: “What specific benefits exist?” Supporting sentence one: “Students can use calendar apps to track assignment due dates.” Supporting sentence two: “They can set reminders for important tasks.”

Step 3: Provide specific examples.

Maria adds detail: “For example, when I walk between classes, I can use my smartphone to record voice notes about research project ideas.”

Step 4: Add a concluding sentence.

Maria’s conclusion: “These organizational tools help students balance their academic and personal responsibilities.”

Maria’s complete paragraph:

Smartphones help students stay organized and manage their time effectively. Students can use calendar apps to track assignment due dates. They can set reminders for important tasks. Note-taking apps allow students to capture ideas anywhere. For example, when I walk between classes, I can use my smartphone to record voice notes about research project ideas. These organizational tools help students balance their academic and personal responsibilities.

Paragraph construction is a process task, which means the learning happens as you make each decision: choosing a topic sentence, selecting which evidence belongs, deciding when the paragraph has said enough. AI can tell you whether a paragraph you have written has one clear idea or several competing ones, but it cannot build that judgment inside you by writing the paragraph for you.

A growth accelerator in this context sounds like this: “Here is a paragraph I wrote. Does it have more than one main idea? If so, where does the paragraph shift? Do not rewrite it, just identify where the focus changes.” You then revise it yourself.

A dependence trap sounds like this: “Rewrite this paragraph so it has a clear topic sentence and better flow.” That prompt transfers the construction work to AI. After several exchanges like that, you will produce tighter paragraphs only as long as AI is present, which is not the same as learning to build them.

Now that you understand how to construct clear sentences and organize them into effective paragraphs, let’s explore the foundation that guides all your writing: the thesis statement.

Lesson 1.3: Developing a Thesis Statement

The thesis statement serves as the key to keeping writing organized. It summarizes your thinking into a single sentence the presents your main insight or argument .

Your thesis statement should answer two simple questions: What topic are you writing about? What position or angle do you take on the topic?

A thesis statement represents a single sentence that provides the answers to these questions clearly and concisely. Ask yourself, “What is my paper about, exactly?” Answering this question will help you develop a precise and direct thesis. This helps not only your reader, but you as well. A clear thesis acts as a roadmap for your own writing process — it keeps your ideas focused, helps you decide what evidence to include, and makes it easier to recognize when a paragraph has drifted away from your main argument.

This is the step where AI use carries its highest risk in academic writing. Asking AI to write a thesis statement for you, or to improve a vague draft into a focused one, produces a result that looks like learning without producing any. The thesis you submit will not reflect your thinking because you did not do the thinking. It will also not hold up once your instructor asks you to develop it into an essay, because you will not know why certain evidence fits and other evidence does not.

A growth accelerator for thesis development sounds like this: “Here is my draft thesis statement. Does it take a clear position on a specific topic, or does it only state a fact? Do not rewrite it, just tell me what is missing.” You then revise it yourself using that feedback. Mateo’s six-step process in the example below shows what this kind of independent development looks like. When you know exactly what you are trying to prove, every sentence has a purpose.

Example Thesis Statements

Let’s examine how successful thesis statements work in practice. You’ll notice each one has both a clear topic and a specific angle:

Thesis: Regular exercise leads to multiple physical and emotional benefits.

  • Topic: Regular exercise
  • Angle: Leads to multiple physical and emotional benefits

Thesis: Requiring school uniforms provides a more equal and less distracting atmosphere at school.

  • Topic: Requiring school uniforms
  • Angle: Provides a more equal and less distracting atmosphere at school

Thesis: Scripture study shared with a friend or family member increases learning.

  • Topic: Scripture study
  • Angle: Shared with a friend or family member increases learning

When you read all of the thesis statements above, can you see areas where the writer could be more specific with their angle? The more specific you are with your topic and claims, the more focused your essay will be for your reader.

A good thesis statement presents one interesting idea, makes a specific claim, and includes supporting evidence.

Practice Example: Developing a Thesis Statement

Let’s follow student Mateo Fernández from Argentina as he develops a thesis statement about online education. You’ll see that creating an effective thesis is a process, not a single moment of inspiration:

Step 1: Identify the broad topic.
  • Mateo’s assignment: Write about the impact of online education.
Step 2: Narrow the topic.
  • Mateo thinks: “Online education seems too broad. What specific aspect interests me?”
  • Mateo’s narrowed topic: Online education for working adults.
Step 3: Determine your position or angle.
  • Mateo asks: “What do I think about online education for working adults?”
  • Mateo’s initial thoughts: It provides convenience and flexibility but also creates challenges.
Step 4: Create a specific angle.
  • Mateo decides to focus on the benefits rather than challenges.
  • Mateo’s angle: Online education provides specific advantages for working adults.
Step 5: Write the thesis statement.
  • Mateo’s first draft: “Online education is good for working adults.”
    • Problem: Too vague and broad.
  • Mateo’s revision: “Online education allows working adults to advance their careers while maintaining family and work responsibilities.”
    • Better: Specific topic plus specific angle.
Step 6: Test the thesis.
  • Mateo asks:
    • “Can I support this with evidence?” Yes.
    • “Is it specific enough?” Yes.
    • “Does it take a clear position?” Yes.
Final thesis:
“Online education allows working adults to advance their careers while maintaining family and work responsibilities.”

This thesis works because it has a clear topic (online education) and a specific angle (career advancement while balancing responsibilities).

Lesson 1.4: Tone, Language, and Audience Adaptation

You already adjust your communication style naturally throughout each day. You probably text differently to your best friend than you do to your professor. You likely speak differently in a job interview than you do at a family dinner. Academic writing simply requires you to be more conscious about these choices.

You can approach your audience’s needs and build common ground with your readers by using three basic approaches in your writing: tone, language, and appeal. Deciding how to adjust your tone and language for a different audience is the rhetorical thinking this lesson is designed to develop. That decision cannot be outsourced to AI without removing the skill entirely. A dependence trap in this context sounds like this: “I need to write this story for my friend and also for a professional audience. What details should I include for each?” That prompt asks AI to perform the audience analysis that the lesson is designed to teach you.

A growth accelerator sounds like this: “I have already decided to use a formal tone for my professional version. Here is a sentence from that version. Does this sentence sound formal or casual, and why?” That prompt asks AI to give you feedback on a decision you already made, and you do the revising yourself. The difference between those two prompts is the difference between practicing a skill and watching someone else practice it for you.

Tone

Tone represents the way you use language to convey your purpose and relationship with your audience. For example, what represents the difference between a formal tone and a conversational tone? For many of your assignments in college, you will write using a formal tone since your audience will be teachers and fellow students. You may use a conversational tone when you write texts and emails to your friends and family.

Tone, Language, and Audience
Audience Tone Language Style
Friends
Texts, personal messages 		Casual, warm relaxed Simple, informal vocabulary, contractions and slang acceptable; personal examples and humor
Professors
Essays, discussion posts, emails 		Formal, respectful, objective Academic vocabulary, no contractions; evidence-based examples; clear thesis and structure
Employers
Cover letters, reports, workplace emails 		Professional, confident, courteous Business language; results-focused; concise and direct; avoids slang and overly technical jargon

Notice the different tones in the two passages below where both managers are contacting their employees about powering down their computers.

Manager 1: It has come to my attention that computers are not being turned off at the end of the workday. What are you thinking? This represents a possible security breach, as well as a waste of electricity. Failure to shut down electronic equipment will NOT be tolerated.

Manager 2: Hello, everyone! Here at Plants, Inc., we remain committed to using energy efficiently. I need your help with respect to our use of computers. A number of computers have been inadvertently left on in the evenings. I want to remind everyone to turn them off before you leave work for the day. Thank you!

Notice the difference in tone between Manager one and Manager two? Most people respond better to polite requests like Manger two’s approach.

Language and Audience

The differences in the kind of language we use depends on our understanding of different audiences. Consider writing an article for a scientific journal. You would use technical language that is appropriate to your subject. Now consider writing a text message to a friend. Your language would be more informal and conversational.

You have several different audiences in your life depending on who you address. Think about your own life for a moment:

  • Writing to friends: Casual tone, simple language, personal examples
  • Writing to professors: Formal tone, academic language, evidence-based examples
  • Writing to employers: Professional tone, business language, results-focused examples

Tone also depends on your professional field. Medical professionals worldwide use specific terminology that transcends cultural boundaries. Software developers from different countries share programming languages and project management terms. International students studying business develop shared vocabulary around concepts like supply chain, market analysis, and financial modeling.

Rhetorical Appeals

Writers use tone and language to connect with an audience. To be persuasive, writers also use what are called rhetorical appeals:

  • Logos - Appeals to logic with facts, statistics, and reasoning
  • Pathos - Appeals to emotions with stories and personal connections
  • Ethos - Appeals to credibility through expertise and trustworthiness
  • A fourth appeal, kairos, the importance of timing, will be introduced in Week 3.

Different audiences respond to different types of appeals. Scientific writing relies heavily on logos. Personal narratives often use pathos. Professional recommendations combine all three.

We’ll explore these concepts in much greater detail in Week 03 of the course.

Tips for Professional Language

Professional communication varies across cultures and industries, but clear principles help you adapt your writing for different global contexts:

  1. Be clear.
    • Complex: “The utilization of teams as a way of optimizing our capacity to meet and prioritize our goals will impact the productivity of the company.”
    • Clear: “Using teams to meet and prioritize our goals will improve company productivity. Teams will execute the goals and enhance the company’s output.”
  2. Match your formality to your context.
    • Informal: “I think anyone who becomes a parent should have to take a parenting class.”
    • Formal: “Parenting classes should be mandatory for any biological or adoptive parents.”
  3. Avoid contractions in formal writing.
    • With contractions: “It shouldn’t be difficult to record what we feel, but many of us just can’t get our feelings down on paper.”
    • Without contractions: “It should not be difficult to record feelings, but many people are unable to do so.”
  4. Use specific language.
    • Vague: “It appears obvious that she represents a sports fan because she makes a really big deal about fans who are totally into the sport.”
    • Specific: “Because of her positive review of dedicated fans, one can assume she supports that team.”

Example: Adapting the Same Story for Different Audiences

Student Siale Vaka from Tonga demonstrates how to adapt personal experiences for different readers. Her story involves transitioning from traditional fishing work to academic study. Notice how she adjusts her approach:

Version 1: Message to Close Friend (excerpt)
I need to tell you about something important that happened this year.

Key Differences Between Versions

Tone: The friend version uses personal, conversational language (“You know how...”), whereas the scholarship version uses formal, professional language (“This transition has shaped...”).

Details: The friend version includes emotions and personal struggles, while the scholarship version focuses on educational development and skills gained.

Language: The friend version uses simple vocabulary and personal examples. In contrast, the scholarship version uses academic terms and professional applications.

Each has the same core story but with different presentations based on audience needs and expectations.

Case Study Follow-Up: How Ada Applied Week 1 Principles

Clear Thesis Statements

Ada’s original thesis:

“There are many factors to consider when examining the question of online education, and various scholars have offered different perspectives on this important topic, which has generated considerable debate in recent years, ultimately suggesting that certain advantages may exist.”

Ada’s revised thesis:

“Online education provides working adults with flexible scheduling, reduced costs, and access to programs unavailable in their geographic areas.”

Ada learned to state her main argument clearly and directly in one focused sentence. She did that revision herself, reading her original thesis, identifying what was vague, and rewriting it word by word, because that process was where the learning happened, not in the polished result it produced.

Sentence Revision

Ada’s original sentence (25 words):

“It might be suggested by some that online education could potentially offer certain benefits to individuals who find themselves in situations where traditional classroom attendance presents various challenges.”

Ada’s revised sentence (11 words):

“Online education benefits students who cannot attend traditional classes.”

Ada learned to eliminate unnecessary words and write more directly.

Audience Adaptation

Ada discovered that Nigerian and American academic writing have different expectations:

Nigerian style: Build relationships and context before stating the main point.

American academic style: State the main point clearly and support it with evidence.

Both approaches are valuable, so Ada learned when and how to use each style.

Transparency does not diminish your work; it demonstrates that you understand what you produced and why. The same principle applies to AI. If you use AI for grammar identification or feedback on a draft you have already written, note it in your submission header: name the tool, describe what it did, and confirm that all ideas and revisions are your own. Students who cannot describe exactly what AI contributed, and what they contributed, have allowed AI to do more than they realize.

Transparency is how you hold yourself accountable for your own learning.

The Result

Ada now helps other international students adapt their writing while preserving their cultural voices.

The writing principles you’ve learned this week of clear thesis statements, strong sentences, effective paragraphs, and audience awareness, can help you communicate successfully across different contexts.

Conclusion

“When we write,” the ancient American prophet Moroni wrote, “we behold our weakness, and stumble because of the placing of our words, and I fear lest the Gentiles shall mock our words” (Ether 12:25). To this, the Lord reminded him that “I make weak things become strong” (Ether 12:27).

Even Moroni, whose words have been quoted and cherished by many, feared the weakness of his writings. You might feel similar concerns about your own writing abilities, but these feelings are natural and show that you care about communicating effectively.

This week you learned four foundational principles that work together to create clear writing.

  • Strong sentences eliminate unnecessary words and communicate directly.
  • Effective paragraphs organize your ideas with a topic sentence and supporting details, each paragraph holding one focused idea.
  • A thesis statement provides direction by stating your main argument in a single, specific sentence.
  • Audience adaptation helps you adjust tone and language for different readers, whether a professor, an employer, or a close friend.

Each of these principles requires you to make decisions about word choice, what evidence belongs, and which tone fits—all areas where learning happens. A polished sentence or a sharper thesis produced by AI is a result, not an education. The effort you invest in these small, deliberate choices this week is what you will carry into every writing task that follows.

We have learned that “by small and simple things are great things brought to pass” (Alma 37:6). Attention to these fundamental principles will strengthen your writing across all contexts including academic papers, personal reflections , and professional communications.

The AI academic honesty policy applies to every assignment this week and every week that follows. Read your course syllabus for any specific AI guidelines your instructor has set. Different assignments have different boundaries. Some tasks permit AI for grammar identification only. Others permit no AI at all. When you are unsure, choose the approach that requires more of your own effort; that choice is always consistent with the spirit of the course, even if the letter of the policy is unclear. Being pressed for time does not change the policy.

Mei, a student in a Family Services program, created a simple chart to track AI policies across her courses when she found they varied from instructor to instructor. She found that adapting to different standards was making her more professional, not less efficient. The same habit will serve you here.

Remember, as a writer, you are also a creator, and this can bring you great joy. Elder Dieter F. Uchtdorf (2008), a modern-day prophet, taught, “Creation brings deep satisfaction and fulfillment. We develop ourselves and others when we take unorganized matter into our hands and mold it into something of beauty.” Writing offers this same creative satisfaction. When you recount life experiences, adapt messages for different audiences, or convey messages for different audiences, you shape meaning from your experience.

Your journey as a writer has just begun. Each time you practice these principles, you build stronger communication skills that will serve you throughout your academic, professional, and personal life. Trust in your ability to improve and remember that even experienced writers continue to learn and grow with every piece they write.

Key Terms

The following terms represent important concepts you encountered throughout this chapter. Review these definitions as needed for assignments and review.

Angle:
Viewpoint or focus

Argument:
Taking a position on an issue and supporting it with evidence audience

Adaptation:
Adjusting writing style, tone, and content to meet different readers’ needs and expectation

Concise:
Expressed in few words; brief and comprehensive

Ethos:
Using credibility or character to appeal to your audience

Evidence:
Acts or observations presented in support of a conclusion or statement

Goal Statement:
Clear expression of future intentions with specific actions and timelines

Language:
Word choice depending on the audience

Logos:
Appealing to your audience’s logical side

Main Idea:
General statement in a paragraph or reading selection

Paragraph:
Building block of writing; a distinct section dealing with a single theme

Pathos:
Appealing to your audience’s emotions

Personal Narrative:
Writing that shares personal experiences and insights with readers

Persuasion:
The action of persuading someone to do or believe something

Reflection:
Thoughtful consideration of experiences and their meaning

Relevant Detail:
Specific information that supports the main point or thesis of a narrative

Rhetorical Appeal:
The qualities of an argument that make it persuasive

Stance:
The attitude of a person or organization toward something; a standpoint

Thesis Statement:
Sentence that provides the topic being discussed and includes the points discussed in the paper

Tone:
The way a writer uses language to communicate a certain attitude

Topic:
The subject of a discourse, argument, or literary composition

Topic Sentence:
A sentence that expresses the main idea of the paragraph in which it occurs

Transition:
A word or phrase that connects one idea to another
